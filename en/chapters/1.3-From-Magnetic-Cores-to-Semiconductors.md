# 1.3 From Magnetic Cores to Semiconductors: Two Storage Revolutions

## You are a needle passing through a magnetic ring

Imagine a scene: In 1965, in a dust-free workshop in Waltham, Massachusetts, USA. Dozens of female workers sat in front of a magnifying lamp, each with a metal frame in front of them. Thousands of sesame-sized magnetic rings were densely arranged on the frame. They used pliers to pick up an enameled wire as thin as a hair. This wire had to be threaded with 64 magnetic rings. There could be no angle deviation or spacing error.

This is not wearing a necklace. This is **Hand Knitting Computer Memory**.

A magnetic ring has only one bit. A 32KB magnetic core memory requires 262,144 magnetic rings. Classic coincident current magnetic cores usually have X/Y drive lines, sense readout lines and inhibitor lines (commonly 4 lines; 3-wire structures need to explain reuse), with a total of about 800,000 threading times. No machine can do this, so it's all done by hand. A skilled female worker wears about a thousand magnetic rings a day, and it takes several months to make a 32KB board.

And if you have a bad spot, for example, a certain magnetic ring is scratched during the threading process, causing magnetic failure, you have no way to hot repair it. You need to cut the thread, pull out the magnetic ring, replace it with a new one, and rethread the thread. It's equivalent to today if a memory is broken and you can't replace the module. You have to take apart the entire memory wire and solder it back one by one.

**This is the manufacturing process of magnetic core memory. It is the electronic device closest to the "textile industry" in human history. **

But it was these hand-woven magnetic rings that carried the Apollo 11 lunar module and landed at the Sea of Tranquility base on the moon on July 20, 1969. They still recorded navigation data and flight instructions intact in the extreme temperature difference of minus 150 degrees to plus 120 degrees, bombarded by cosmic rays without attenuation, and violent vibrations during the launch of the Saturn V rocket. When Neil Armstrong said "That's one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind", the flight parameters corresponding to this sentence lay quietly in those handmade magnetic rings.

Why did NASA choose a "hand-woven" thing for storage of the lunar landing computer? Why not more advanced semiconductors?

Because the magnetic core has an overwhelming quality that no other technology at the time had: it is non-volatile and radiation-resistant. **

## How does a magnetic ring remember a bit?

The principle of magnetic core memory is shockingly simple. It’s an intuitive physical phenomenon that you don’t even need to study electrical engineering to understand:

```
The physics of magnetic core memory

         ┌──────────────┐
         │ Ferrite magnetic ring │ ← Diameter about 1mm, sintered body of magnetic material
    ─────┼──┐ ┌──┼───── X line (drive line)
         │ │ │ │
    ─────┼──┼────────┼──┼─────Y line (drive line)
         │ │ ┌───┐ │ │
    ─────┼──┼──│ S │─┼──┼───── Readout line (sensing line)
         │ │ └───┘ │ │
         └──┼────────┼──┘
            └────────┘

How it works:
Write "1" → X/Y lines are energized at the same time, resulting in a combined magnetic field > coercive force → the magnetic ring is magnetized clockwise
Write "0" → the reverse current cooperates with the inhibit line to suppress flipping; the simplified 3-line diagram does not draw the inhibitor
Read → Apply "write 0" current → If originally "1", the magnetization flips → An induced current pulse is generated on the read line
        If it is originally "0", the magnetization does not turn → there is no pulse in the readout line
        ⚠ Reads are destructive! After reading, it must be written back to the original value.
```

Note: Classic coinciding-current cores usually also require inhibitor lines (commonly 4 lines); the figure shows a simplified 3-line drawing.

This ferrite toroid has a rectangular hysteresis loop, which is a key characteristic. Ordinary magnetic materials become magnetized when you add a magnetic field, and demagnetize when you remove the magnetic field. But the ferrite magnetic ring is different: if you add a magnetic field above the critical point, it will flip; after the magnetic field is removed, it will retain the magnetization direction unless you add a magnetic field in the opposite direction.

This is a natural, purely physical "latch". It does not need to be powered on, does not need to be refreshed, and is not affected by power outages. It is just a small magnet. When the magnet is energized, it flips its direction, and the direction remains unchanged after the power is turned off.

The reason why the read operation generates pulse signals relies on Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction: the changing magnetic field induces an electromotive force in the wire. When the magnetization direction is reversed, the magnetic flux passing through the magnetic ring changes drastically, and a momentary current appears on the readout line. If the magnetization direction is not reversed (it is originally "0"), the magnetic flux does not change, and there is no pulse.

This principle is a minimalist presentation of the idea of ​​**differential signals**. Don't look at the absolute value, just look at "whether there is any change". Today's high-speed serial communications (USB, PCIe) still use the same idea, by detecting the voltage flip of the differential line to determine the data, but the magnetic core uses magnetic flux flip, while the modern bus uses the current direction flip.Destructive reading is the most significant disadvantage of magnetic cores. The data must be "destroyed" and written back every time it is read, which means that the reading process is a read-modify-write sequence, not an atomic operation. If power is lost between read-modify-write, the data will be permanently lost. This problem also existed in the early days of Flash: NOR Flash reading is not destructive, but erasing must occur before programming. Power interruption during the erasing process may leave the data block in an undefined state. Therefore, destructive reading of magnetic cores is a problem of "non-atomic operations" at the semantic level, and the spiritual descendants of this problem can be seen in every generation of storage media. **

## Wiring topology of core matrix

If you just put three wires through a magnetic ring, you can only store one bit. How does a real magnetic core memory use hundreds of thousands of threading operations to weave a randomly addressable memory matrix?

```
Matrix topology of magnetic core memory (4×4 = 16 bit)

              X0 X1 X2 X3
              │ │ │ │
         ┌────┼────┼────┼────┼────┐
     Y0 ─┼────●────●────●────●────┼───
         │ │ │ │ │ │
     Y1 ─┼────●────●────●────●────┼───
         │ │ │ │ │ │
     Y2 ─┼────●────●────●────●────┼───
         │ │ │ │ │ │
     Y3 ─┼────●────●────●────●────┼───
         └────┼────┼────┼────┼────┘
              │ │ │ │
           Readout wire (snake through all magnetic rings)
```

There is a magnetic ring at each intersection point where the X and Y lines coincide. To select a specific magnetic ring (say coordinates (2, 1)), you feed half the drive current to the X2 line and the other half to the Y1 line. All other rings only sense half the current in one wire, which is not enough to flip the magnetization direction. Only the magnetic ring at position (2, 1) feels the superposition of two half-currents, which just crosses the coercive force threshold.

This is the **Coincident Current Selection** technology, which uses 2N lines to manage N² units, and each unit is addressed through current superposition. It is the ancestor of "crossed addressing of word lines + bit lines" in memory chips. In today's DRAM chips, a 4Gb chip relies on row address and column address to interleave to select a memory unit. It's just that the magnetic core uses wires, and the DRAM uses MOS tubes. The topology is the same.

What about the signal strength sensed during readout? The voltage generated on the readout line when a magnetic ring flips is only a few tens of millivolts. The readout line has to snake through thousands of magnetic rings, and the induced noise may be greater than the signal. Therefore, the readout line must be connected to the **differential/readout amplifier**, and the flip signal can be extracted after comparing it with the reference level.

The combination of these three technologies, ferrite materials, coincident current selection, and differential amplification, allowed computers in the 1950s to have "large-capacity" randomly addressable memory ranging from several thousand to tens of thousands of words. The first computer to use magnetic cores was MIT's Memory Test Computer; Whirlwind was put into operation in 1951 and replaced with magnetic cores in about 1953. The cores are hand-strung by an MIT team and trained workers (mostly women).

## The Legacy of Magnetic Cores: The Golden Age of Non-Volatile

Magnetic core memory dominated the field of computer memory for nearly two decades, from the 1950s to the early 1970s. It is installed on IBM's mainframe, DEC's PDP minicomputer, and NASA's spacecraft. Then, in the 1970s, it suddenly disappeared.

The reason for its disappearance is that it cannot be automated.

Semiconductor circuits can be manufactured in batches using a photolithography process. On a silicon wafer, hundreds or even thousands of transistors are etched using chemicals and lasers. Thousands of chips can be produced in one process. Each magnetic ring of the magnetic core memory must be threaded manually. No matter how optimized this kind of labor is, it cannot be infinitely accelerated like a machine.

But the legacy left by the magnetic core has been inherited:

1. **Non-volatile**. The core's non-volatility comes from the persistence of the physical magnetization, which has no concept of "leakage" because there is no ongoing dependence between magnetization and current. This idea was reborn in Flash. Floating-gate transistors replaced magnetization with charges isolated by an oxide layer. The insulation of the oxide layer ensures that charges cannot leak away at will.

2. **Writeback logic after destructive read**. Data must be recovered after the core is read, and the act of reading itself has side effects. This model is reborn in DRAM, where read operations also consume the charge on the capacitor and must be written back. A design that seemed to be a shortcoming turned out to be a pattern that was repeatedly used in later storage architectures: Today's NAND Flash reads will interfere with adjacent cells and require regular "read interference recovery."3. **Staggered and Radiation Protection**. The radiation resistance of the magnetic core is still missed by aerospace and military electronics. High-energy particles in space can penetrate silicon chips, flip SRAM storage nodes, and penetrate the gate oxide layer of MOS tubes. However, to flip the macroscopic magnetization direction of a ferrite magnetic ring, a cosmic particle requires much more energy than flipping a semiconductor node. The magnetic core resists quantum effects on a physical scale (millimeter level). This "big as a shield" idea is anti-trend in modern times, but as we will see later, in order to protect data in Flash, humans have invented extremely complex error-correcting codes (ECC), which is equivalent to replacing physical resistance with digital algorithms. The magnetic core directly uses materials to solve the problem, and Flash uses mathematics to solve the same problem, achieving the same goal through different approaches.

```
Magnetic Core vs. Flash: Resonances half a century apart

                        Core (1950s) Flash (1980s)
Non-volatile principle, magnetization direction is persistent, floating gate charge is persistent
Writing mechanism Current flip magnetization Hot electron injection/FN tunneling
Reading mechanism Electromagnetic induction pulse Threshold voltage detection
Destructive read?            Yes (needs writeback) No, but there is read interference
Random access?              Completely random Completely random (NOR) / Page access (NAND)
Minimum cell size: about 1mm, about 10nm (3D NAND)
Manufacturing method: hand weaving, photolithography + chemical etching
Radiation resistance is extremely strong and requires ECC assistance
```

## The birth of DRAM: a transistor plus a capacitor

In 1966, Robert Dennard was sitting in his office at IBM's Thomas Watson Research Center thinking about a seemingly simple question: Can a storage unit be made with fewer parts?

At that time, SRAM (static RAM) required six transistors to store one bit. It was a typical bistable flip-flop circuit, with two cross-coupled inverters ensuring that the state was "locked". Six transistors take up a lot of silicon area and are very costly, limiting the storage capacity on a single chip.

Dennard's inspiration was an extreme subtraction: **a transistor, a capacitor. **

```
DRAM memory cell (1T1C)

    ┌────Bit Line
    │
    ├────┬── N-channel MOSFET (switching transistor)
    │ │
    │ │ ┌─── Storage capacitor
    │ │ │ Cs ≈ 20-30 fF (flying method)
    │ │ │
    │ │ │
    │ word line │
    └─────────┴──── GND
    (Word Line)
```

How it works:
- A high voltage is applied to the word line → the transistor is turned on → the voltage on the bit line charges the capacitor (writes "1") or discharges it (writes "0")
- The word line is driven low → the transistor is turned off → the charge on the capacitor is isolated and should theoretically remain permanently

The problem is: it doesn't hold up.

That capacitor is only 20-30 fF (10⁻¹⁵ Farads). What is this concept? The capacitor you make with two metal films separated by a few microns is tens of femtofarads. With such a small capacitance, even if there is a turned-off transistor between it and the outside world, it will leak current at an extremely slow speed. The transistor is not a perfect insulator when it is turned off, and its sub-threshold leakage is usually in the fA–pA level. Taking a 25fF capacitor as an example, 1pA leakage drops 1V in about 25ms, and the holding time is on the order of tens of milliseconds.

Therefore, the "D" of DRAM means Dynamic, which means dynamic. It does not refer to its dynamic performance, but refers to the dynamic decay of its storage state. If you don't refresh it, it will be forgotten. Never remember. DRAM is not "poor memory", it is "complete amnesia". The length of time its memory exists depends on how often you feed it energy.

This fatal flaw was "covered up" by an exquisite architecture.

## The Magic of Refresh: Use Speed to Buy Continuity

The DRAM chip has a built-in refresh counter that periodically reads out, amplifies, and writes back each row of data. In a standard DDR4 chip, each row must be refreshed every 64 milliseconds. If the refresh frequency is not enough, data will be corrupted, bits will flip, and the system will crash.

Let's do a very intuitive conversion:

```
A standard cache line for DDR4-3200
===========================
The line buffer is usually about 1KB (8192 bit)
Refresh interval: 64ms (time window for all rows to be refreshed)
Refresh time for each row: about 350ns

One line of "charged life": 64ms (the time from the last refresh to when the voltage decays to the point where it cannot be judged)
But a row of "data life": as long as it is still within the refresh cycle → infinite

Essence = trading bandwidth for memory
Refresh overhead varies with capacity/tRFC, typically around 2%–5%
```

The refresh circuit works silently in the background and never stops. It urges every line of data as rhythmically as the beating of a heart, "Wake up and don't forget who you are." If the computer suddenly loses power while it is running, the DRAM forgets everything within milliseconds and all data is lost forever.

From this perspective, DRAM is not a storage medium, it is a simulation of persistence. It itself has no physical basis for continuous existence (unlike the permanent magnet of the magnetic core, the charge is destined to leak), but it maintains a "seemingly permanent" state through high-frequency refresh, which is an artificially injected energy flow.

This is a deception, but it is effective enough to allow you to write a book, render a movie, and run a deep learning training on your computer. The intermediate states of all these operations are in the form of capacitive charges and are protected by the refresh circuit between microsecond-scale periodic life and death.

## Cave dwellings and tents—two types of storage that are on the opposite side of FlashWithin the "library" metaphor, let's situate the characters we've seen so far:

**Magnetic core is a stone cellar library. ** Digging into the Loess Plateau does not require much material or fine processing, just a hole. However, this cave is warm in winter and cool in summer, with a natural constant temperature, and the bookshelf will not collapse easily even if there is a power outage. It relies on the physical nature of the material, that is, the compressive strength and inertia of the soil, to resist external damage. You don't need to "moisture-proof" it, it's inherently inert. But stone cellars cannot be moved and cannot be scaled up. You cannot use one mold to mass-produce stone cellars. Each one must be excavated by hand in a specific mountain of soil. The magnetic core is exactly that: non-volatile + anti-interference comes from the physical properties of the material, but hand-weaving is destined to be unable to compete with the scale of semiconductors.

**DRAM is a tent reading room. ** You can build it (one transistor + one capacitor) at a very small cost, you can move it, you can build it again, and you can build many of them. But the tent is not wind-resistant. If a strong wind blows, you need to keep holding it and tightening it (refreshing). As long as you don't stop maintaining it, the tent will never fall down; as soon as you let go, the tent will fall down when the strong wind comes, and all the books will be scattered on the ground. After a power outage, the data evaporates completely without any trace.

The next one to appear is **Flash, a reinforced concrete library building. ** It utilizes the most advanced contemporary manufacturing process (silicon lithography technology), designs memory cells at the molecular level of the material (floating gate + oxide layer isolation), is both non-volatile (not lost when powered off) and rewriteable (programming and erasing), and can be scaled to a single chip with a capacity of hundreds of GB. **Flash merges the non-volatility of a stone cellar with the manufacturability of a tent, but at a price: it must face its own new "original sin", which is wear and tear and power-off reliability. **

## EPROM: The first appearance of floating gate

In 1970, Intel had just been established for two years, and its main product was SRAM chips. An engineer named Dov Frohman noticed a strange phenomenon while studying the failure of an SRAM chip: some transistors would experience a drift in threshold voltage under certain conditions. This drift seemed to be related to the charge accumulated on the gate, and these charges did not disappear after power was turned off.

He realized that this was not a "failure", it was a new discovery.

In 1971, Intel released the 1702 EPROM, mankind's first floating-gate memory chip. Its storage unit structure is as follows:

```
EPROM memory cell (FAMOS transistor)

    ┌─────────┬──── Control gate (none! 1702 EPROM has no control gate)
    │ │
    │ ┌──────┴─── SiO₂ insulating layer
    │ │ ┌────── Floating gate (polysilicon, completely wrapped by the oxide layer, no electrical connection to the outside world)
    │ │ │
  ┌┴──┴──┴──┐
  │ Source │ Drain
  │P │P
  └────┬─────┘
       │
    N type substrate
```

The floating gate is a thin layer of polysilicon completely surrounded by silicon dioxide, an extremely excellent insulator. There is no conductive path between it and the outside world, wires cannot be connected, and current cannot reach it. It is a "sealed" space in the physical sense. But if you add a high enough voltage (about 25V) between the source and drain, the high-energy electrons will "bump" through the barrier of the oxide layer. This is **hot carrier injection**, and the electrons will enter the floating gate and be permanently trapped.

When electrons are trapped in the floating gate, a fixed electric field is added to the channel area, changing the threshold voltage of the transistor, and the on/off voltage point moves. Every time you read this transistor in the future, you apply an intermediate voltage. If there is charge in the floating gate, the tube will not pass; if there is no charge in the floating gate, the tube will conduct. **Physical state, encoded as a shift in threshold voltage. **

Writing requires a high voltage of 25V and about 50 milliseconds, but reading only requires 5V. The charge in the floating gate can theoretically be maintained for decades.

What about erasure?

**UV** is required for erasure.

Yes, you need to cut a quartz window into the chip's package. To erase data, you expose the chip to a UV lamp for about 20 minutes. High-energy ultraviolet photons provide enough energy for the electrons in the floating gate to escape, and the electrons jump out of the floating gate and return to the substrate through the photoionization effect. All floating gates are cleared at the same time, that is, all are erased without selectivity. **

This is EPROM's true "programmable read-only memory": it can be written, but it can only be completely erased and then rewritten. You cannot change just one byte, you must erase the whole chip and then write it from scratch.

## EEPROM: Electrical erasure is finally hereIn 1978, George Perlegos invented EEPROM, also known as Electrically Erasable PROM, at Intel (or Intel, because the EPROM team has been pushing in this direction).

The core breakthrough is to add an ultra-thin oxide layer (about 10nm) to the floating-gate transistor. At this thickness, a voltage much lower than 25V (about 12V) can generate a strong enough electric field to allow electrons to directly pass through the oxide layer through **Fowler-Nordheim tunneling** without the need for ultraviolet photons or hot carriers.

However, the tunneling current is extremely small, so the erasing speed is very slow. Only one byte can be erased in a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds.

EEPROM is electrically erasable at the byte level, but this means that each memory cell on the chip requires a pair of selection transistors to isolate the erase high voltage. The unit area is huge, the capacity is extremely small (early EEPROMs were only a few KB), and the cost is expensive. It is destined to be unable to do large-capacity storage.

A detail remembered by history: One of the early applications of EEPROM was **preset channel storage for car radios**. Your preset stations will not disappear after the ignition is turned off, which was not possible before EEPROM (unless magnetic core or battery-backed SRAM was used). An electrical engineer's late-night worry, "Why do I have to re-adjust the channel every time I drive?" has promoted the commercialization of a semiconductor technology.

## Fujio Masuoka: The Genius Let Down by Toshiba

Now we come to the protagonist of this story. In 1980, in Toshiba's Semiconductor Division, a 37-year-old engineer sat in the laboratory and frowned at a pile of EEPROM design drawings.

His name is Fujio Masuoka. He looked at the cell circuit of EEPROM. Each memory cell requires two transistors, one to store data and one to isolate high voltage during erasure. He had only one idea:

**Can the selection transistor be omitted as well? **

EEPROM cannot be without a selector tube, because the erase voltage must be accurately controlled for each byte. If you connect the entire row or even the entire floating gate to the same erase line, the uniformity of the electric field will be extremely poor. Some cells will not be wiped clean, and some cells will be damaged by over-erasing.

Shi Gang's idea is the opposite: since a single erasure has such high requirements on the circuit, then just erase the whole block. The granularity of erasure is sacrificed in exchange for the simplicity of the storage unit.

This is the birth of NOR Flash.

```
NOR Flash memory unit

    ┌───────────── Control gate (CG)
    │ ┌─────────── Floating gate (FG)
    │ │
    │ │ ┌─────── ONO interlayer insulation (oxide-nitride-oxide)
    │ │ │ ┌──── Tunnel oxide layer (about 10nm)
    │ │ │ │
  ┌┴──┴──┴──┴──┐
  │ Source │ Drain
  │ n⁺ │ n⁺
  └──────┬──────┘
         │
    P type substrate

Write: High voltage is applied to the drain → hot electrons are injected into the floating gate
Erase: High voltage is applied to the source → FN tunneling pulls electrons from the floating gate
Read: control gate plus read voltage → detection threshold voltage
```

Pay attention to this unit: it has only one transistor. There is no selection tube, no isolation tube, and no additional circuitry. It is just a floating gate, a control gate, and a tunnel oxide layer. This is the **simplest structure** Flash unit. The EEPROM of the same period required two tubes to achieve the function, so Sugaoka used one tube.

But the price is: it cannot be byte erased, it must be sector erased, and a sector is usually 4KB–64KB cleared at the same time.

This is not a bug, this is a feature. Sugaoka is willing to give up the granularity of byte erasure in exchange for a leap in storage density. For scenarios where code storage is the main focus (firmware, startup code), sector erasure is not a problem at all. You will never update just one byte of firmware, you will refresh the entire sector together.

NOR Flash is named after its memory cell matrix topology: the drain of each cell is directly connected in parallel to a bit line, similar to the parallel input of a NOR gate. You can randomly access any address, and like a NOR gate, it will give a result when any input is high. NOR Flash supports **direct execution of code on the chip (XIP, eXecute In Place)**. The CPU can directly read instructions from Flash and execute them without copying them to RAM first.

This is revolutionary in the embedded field. You do not need large-capacity RAM to preload program code, the CPU can run directly from Flash.

## NAND Flash: The "lazy man" invention that changed the worldNOR Flash succeeded. But Shuogang didn't stop.

Although the NOR Flash unit is a single-tube unit, each unit requires an independent bit line contact and a source contact. The metal contacts occupy a large area, which limits the further improvement of storage density.

Shigaoka asked another "lazy person" question: Can a string of memory cells be connected in series and share bit lines and sources?

In 1987, the answer was:

```
NAND Flash storage string (NAND String)

bit line
 │
 ├── String selector (SSL)
 │
 ├── WL0 ──┬── Memory cell (floating gate transistor)
 ├── WL1 ──┼── Storage unit
 ├── WL2 ──┼── Storage unit
 │ ... │ ...
 ├── WLn ──┼── Storage unit
 │
 ├── Ground selection tube (GSL)
 │
 ┴
GND
```

In a string of NAND String, dozens (later hundreds) of memory cells are connected in series end to end, sharing a bit line contact and a source contact. The entire string of memory cells only requires two selection tubes + two metal contacts, while each unit of NOR Flash requires independent metal contacts.

The trade-off: you can't randomly access arbitrary cells in the NAND string. You must drive the gate voltage of all unselected cells in the entire string to "on" (higher than the highest threshold voltage), and then place the word line of the target cell at an intermediate read voltage. Whether current can flow through the entire series link depends on whether the target cell is turned on. **To access a bit, you need to open other people on the entire path first. **

But what if it can’t be accessed randomly? Storage is full page read and write. You can read an entire page (such as 16KB) at a time, cache it in a register, and then access it arbitrarily from the register.

**At the expense of random access, the area of ​​a single unit of NAND Flash is reduced to less than a quarter of that of NOR Flash. **

In 1987, Masuoka published a paper on NAND Flash at Toshiba's IEEE International Electronic Devices Meeting (IEDM). The whole place was quiet. Then there were whispers and nods.

## The invention that missed the Nobel Prize

Toshiba's reward for Sugaoka: a few hundred dollars.

No joke. Seokoka recalled in his autobiography that the company gave him a bonus of about a few hundred dollars after inventing Flash, an invention that changed the face of human information technology and was worth no more than a high-end Japanese meal.

Toshiba had no confidence in Flash at that time and put its resource center on DRAM, because DRAM is the pride of the Japanese semiconductor industry. The five giants NEC, Toshiba, Hitachi, Fujitsu, and Mitsubishi dominate the global DRAM market. Flash is seen as a strange invention that "doesn't know what it can do."

Sugaoka's situation at Toshiba became increasingly marginalized. He later recalled: In the laboratory, other engineers saw him and took a detour: "Look, the madman who makes Flash is here again."

The ending of this story is very dramatic: Flash was ignored at Toshiba, but its value was discovered by Intel in the United States. Intel launched a commercial product of NOR Flash in 1988 to store BIOS firmware of PCs, and it has skyrocketed since then. Although the invention of NAND Flash was preempted by Toshiba, Toshiba released NAND in 1987 and promoted commercialization; Samsung mass-produced it in the 1990s. In the early 1990s, Samsung invested heavily in developing NAND Flash mass production technology. By the 2000s, Samsung became the world's largest NAND Flash manufacturer.

Fujio Shioka transferred to Tohoku University in 1994 and served as CTO of Unisantis in 2004. No stock dividends, no huge bonuses, no patent compensation that should at least be sent. Decades later, when a reporter interviewed him, he smiled bitterly and said: **"If my NAND Flash paper had been destroyed by the company instead of being allowed to be published, Toshiba might still be the same Toshiba."**

He likens his technical career to "an eagle that grew up in a chicken coop and has never flown but knows it can fly."Now, that photo you took casually on your phone is lying in the NAND Flash invented by Shugang. There are six billion mobile phones, and the memory of each mobile phone is a descendant of Sungang's NAND Flash. From the "lazy man's" idea in 1987, that is, "Can we also save the selection tube and string the units together?" to today's mass production technology of 232 layers of 3D NAND stacking, Shiokang's invention has become the most produced semiconductor device in human history.

## Library: Stone Cellar, Tent and Archives

Now let us use the metaphor of "library" to complete this journey from magnetic core to Flash.

The magnetic core is a stone cellar library. You can dig directly into the mountain, and physical inertia provides a natural guarantee for non-volatility and data durability. Amid the vibrations of rocket launches, the bombardment of space radiation, and the environment of one hundred degrees below zero, this stone cellar is as stable as a rock. But you can’t dig many. Each stone cellar requires unique mountains and manpower: the essence of manual threading is that it is a work of art without drawings, which cannot be copied or mass-produced.

DRAM is a tent reading room. It is so light that you can set it up with two poles (a transistor + a capacitor), and you can set up millions of tents on one square centimeter of land. But the nature of the tent is unstable. You must have someone to hold it continuously (refresh the circuit). If you don't leave it for even a moment, the tent will collapse and all the books (data) will be scattered. It has no independent persistence; its persistence is entirely dependent on the continuous infusion of external energy.

And Flash is a reinforced concrete library building. It is the crystallization of the manufacturing capabilities of human industrial civilization. Cement (silica, SiO₂) is a high-purity refined product of sand, and steel bar (polycrystalline silicon floating gate) is another form of sand. From silicon wafer to chip, it is a set of extremely precise processes (photolithography, ion implantation, oxidation, chemical mechanical polishing). Flash is one of the purest materials on earth (the impurity content of chip-level SiO₂ is measured in parts per billion). This library building is non-volatile (the collection will not be lost in the event of a power outage), can be rewritten (clearing a floor of bookshelves and renumbering them), and can be built with hundreds of layers (the number of stacked layers of 3D NAND has soared from 24 layers in 2013 to 232 layers and above today). But it has its limits: Reinforced concrete is not eternal, it will wear out (the upper limit of the erasure cycle) and will age (the data retention time shortens with the increase in wear and tear).

The stone cellar is resistant to radiation but not large-scale, the tent is large-scale but not resistant to power outages, and the archive building is both but introduces the sword of Damocles of "wear" that always hangs overhead.

From handmade magnetic rings in the 1950s to 232-layer 3D NAND in the 2020s, human storage technology has gone through a journey from "physical inertia" to "architectural magic". Early technology relied on the physical properties of the material itself to ensure data security. The magnetic core relied on macroscopic magnetization, and EPROM relied on electrons blocked by SiO₂. Later technologies rely more and more on the cover-up of the "architectural layer". For example, DRAM relies on refresh, and Flash relies on FTL + wear leveling + ECC + power-down protection to make unreliable physical media present an illusion of "reliability" to users.

And this "illusion" is the theme of our next section: the original sin of storage.
