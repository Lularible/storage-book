# 3.7 Wear Leveling: Aging Every Shelf Evenly

## You are the lord of a piece of land, and the land can only be cultivated one hundred thousand times.

Imagine a scenario: You are the lord of an agricultural society with 128 fields on your territory. Your tenant farmers need to grow food on these 128 fields. But there is a harsh law of nature: each field can only be cultivated 100,000 times. After planting 100,000 times, the field becomes useless and no more crops can grow.

And your tenant farmers have a habit: they always go to the field closest to them, which is field No. 1. Field No. 1 is plowed, sown and harvested every day, 365 days a year, without a day falling. Field No. 100 is too far away, no one goes there, and it is overgrown with weeds.

100,000 times ÷ 365 days ≈ 274 years. It looks like you don't need to worry. But your tenant farmer has to go in and out of Field No. 1 dozens of times a day, far more than just farming once a day. Soon, Field No. 1 had been plowed 80,000 times and cracks began to appear. Field No. 100 has only been plowed 300 times and is almost virgin land.

Your field number 1 is dying. Once Field No. 1 is completely destroyed, the crops originally on Field No. 1 will have to be transplanted to other fields, increasing the burden on all fields. Moreover, your territory has a total of 128 fields. Field No. 1 was abandoned, leaving only 127 pieces. Then cracks began to appear in field No. 2...

**This is the death scenario for a Flash file system without wear leveling.**

## Hot data and cold data: class differentiation on Flash

In the world of Flash, data is divided into hot and cold, and this is a precise engineering classification.

```
Hot data vs cold data
=======================

Hot Data:
  - Frequently modified: log files, configuration files, system status
  - may be written several times per second
  - The occupied physical blocks are frequently erased → wear out quickly
  - Example: ECU operation log, system clock synchronization record

Cold Data:
  - Almost no modification: firmware, calibration data, font files
  - It may have been written and never changed.
  - Occupied physical blocks are almost never erased → wear slowly
  - Example: bootloader, product serial number

Warm Data:
  - Occasional modifications: user configuration, calibration parameter corrections
  - between hot and cold
  - Examples: Calibration data tables, user preferences
```

Without wear leveling, the block where the hot data is located will be "burned" and reach the P/E Cycle limit within a few days, while the block where the cold data is located is almost new.

```
Hot data death graph without wear leveling
=======================

Block 0 (SB0): 99,500 times
Block 1 (SB1): 98,200 times
Block 2 (data): ████████████████████████████████ 88,000 times
Block 3 (data): █████████████████████████ 61,000 times
Block 4 (Configuration Log): ██████████████████████████████████████ 100,000 times ← Killed!
Block 5 (firmware block): █ 300 times
Block 6 (firmware block): █ 100 times
...
Block 127 (font): █ 5 times

Wear imbalance ratio = 100,000 ÷ 5 = 20,000 times
```

This extreme imbalance means: **The life of the entire Flash chip = the life of the hottest piece of data.** What you buy is a Flash with a nominal 100,000 P/E times, but the actual service life you get is limited by hot data, and may only use less than 1% of the total erase and write capacity (100,000 × 128 = 12,800,000 erase and write times).

## Two schools of wear leveling

Wear leveling engineering techniques fall into two main schools.

**School 1: Dynamic Wear Leveling**

```
Dynamic wear leveling algorithm
=======================

find_free_block():
  1. Traverse the free bitmap (free_bitmap)
  2. For all free blocks (blocks with bit=0), check their wear_count
  3. Return the block with the smallest wear_count
  4. After the allocation is successful, the wear_count of the block +1

Example:
  Free block list: Block 7(wear=500), Block 12(wear=200), Block 89(wear=6800)
  → find_free_block() returns block 12 (wear minimum)
  → Block 12 is written and wear becomes 201

Principle: Each time a new block is allocated, choose the one with the least wear.
This ensures that all blocks in the free block pool age evenly.
```

Dynamic wear leveling is passive. It does not actively move data, but only makes "optimal choices" when allocating new blocks. Its advantages are:
- Simple: only one more comparison is needed during allocation
- Low overhead: no additional erase or write operations are required
- Small RAM requirements: only the wear_count array is required (128 × 4 = 512 bytes)

The Achilles heel of dynamic wear leveling is: **It cannot move cold data.** If block 5 stores a copy of the firmware (cold data, almost never modified), the wear_count of block 5 may stay at 300 times forever. While other blocks are frequently allocated and erased. If you don't touch block 5, block 5 will not participate in the equilibrium.

```
The blind spots of dynamic wear leveling
=======================

Block 5 (firmware, wear=300): █ ← cold data, does not participate in balancing
Block 6 (firmware, wear=100): █ ← cold data
...
Block 40 (log, wear=80,000): ███████████████████████████████████
Block 41 (log, wear=82,000): ███████████████████████████████████
Block 42 (idle, wear=78,000): ██████████████████████████████████
Block 43 (idle, wear=79,500): ██████████████████████████████████

Dynamic wear leveling can only make the free block pool (blocks 42, 43...) even
But nothing can be done about the occupied cold data blocks (blocks 5, 6)
They occupy low-wear positions but do not participate in write cycles
```

**School 2: Static Wear Leveling**

```
Static wear leveling algorithm
=======================

Periodic inspection:
  1. Find the occupied block (cold data block) with the smallest wear_count
  2. Find the block with the largest wear_count (usually in the free pool)
  3. If the difference exceeds a threshold (e.g. max_wear - min_wear > 10,000):
     a. Move the data of the cold data block to a high-wear free block
     b. Release the physical location of the cold data block → enter the free pool
     c. Cold data blocks (now free and with low wear) can be used by hot data

Example:
  Cold block 5 (firmware): wear=300, data="firmware_v2.1.bin"
  Hot block 99 (idle): wear=85,000

  Relocation:
    1. Allocate a buffer and read the data of block 5
    2. Write data to block 99 (new location)
    3. Update file table: firmware is now at block 99
    4. Erase block 5 → block 5 becomes a free block (wear=301)
    5. Block 5 is now available for hot data → wear rate starts to rise
```

Static wear leveling is proactive and moves cold data around at all costs, forcing them into a wear cycle. The cost is obvious:
- Additional erase and write operations (moving data = erase once + program once)
- The algorithm is more complex (requires periodic scanning of the global wear distribution)
- May trigger at inappropriate times (increases uncertainty in system latency)

```
The cost of static wear leveling
=======================

Physical cost of moving a 4KB cold data block:
  1. Read cold data block → 82 microseconds (NOR Flash read latency)
  2. Erase target block → 200 milliseconds (NOR Flash erase delay)
  3. Program target block → 58 milliseconds (NOR Flash programming delay)
  4. Update metadata → 1 SB Log append + trigger Compact when necessary

  Total physical overhead: ~258 ms + read time
  P/E overhead: target block wear +1, cold data block wear +1 (erasure)

  Additional wear was done purely for the sake of "balance".
  This is called a Wear Leveling Tax.
```

## Wear leveling design of KnotFS: dynamic as the main component, static as the supplement

In KnotFS, the implementation of wear leveling is divided into three levels.

**First layer: persistence of wear_count array**

This is wear leveling infrastructure, you have to know how many times each block has been wiped.

```c
// Represents pseudocode (not current knotfs.c)
#define KNOTFS_BLOCK_COUNT  (16)

// Each block corresponds to a 32-bit counter (actually sb.wear[])
extern uint32_t g_wear_count[KNOTFS_BLOCK_COUNT];

// Key issue: wear_count itself must also be saved in Flash
// But storing wear_count also needs to be erased → The process of updating wear_count will cause wear!
// This is a "reflexive question"
```

It is this "reflexivity problem", that is, the operation of recording wear information itself generates wear, that makes the engineering implementation of wear leveling tricky.

The actual strategy of KnotFS is: **wear[] is a field of SB Header, and is only written to Flash along with the entire 668-byte SB during compaction. SB Log only carries 8-byte bitmap records and does not carry wear snapshots.**

```
Persistence strategy for wear_count
=======================

Not:
  Each erase → update wear_count immediately → write to Flash
  To do this: wear_count own write frequency = erase frequency
  The wear counting operation itself wears as much as normal operation!

Instead:
  Accumulated wear changes over time
  When the metadata transaction is committed (for example, after writing the file is completed)
  When compacting, write the entire 668-byte SB containing wear[]
  Precisely defined wear count loss ≤ number of erases since last commit
```

**Second layer: dynamic balancing of find_free_block()**

This is the workhorse of KnotFS wear leveling.

```c
// Simplified pseudocode for find_free_block()
uint32 find_free_block(void) {
    uint32 best_block = FS_INVALID_BLOCK;
    uint32 min_wear = 0xFFFFFFFF;  // Initialize a huge value

    for (uint32 i = 0; i < FS_BLOCK_COUNT; i++) {
        // Only free blocks are considered (free_bitmap[i] == 0)
        if (g_free_bitmap[i] == 0) {
            if (g_wear_count[i] < min_wear) {
                min_wear = g_wear_count[i];
                best_block = i;
            }
        }
    }

    if (best_block != FS_INVALID_BLOCK) {
        g_wear_count[best_block]++;  // Wear and tear is taken into account when allocated
        g_free_bitmap[best_block] = 1;  // Mark as occupied
    }

    return best_block;
}
```

The complexity of this algorithm is O(n). The above pseudocode uses 128 blocks as an example; the current KnotFS is 16 blocks, the number of comparisons is fewer, and the overhead is negligible.

**Third layer: GC victim strategy in design (KnotFS currently has no GC)**

If GC is implemented, the following victim selection strategy can be used; KnotFS currently does not have independent GC.
1. Blocks with the most garbage (maximizing recycling efficiency)
2. If the amount of garbage is the same → choose the one with the highest wear count (helps static balancing)

```
GC Victim selection strategy
=======================

Candidate block A: 80% garbage, wear=50,000
Candidate block B: 80% garbage, wear=12,000

First sort by the amount of garbage → both are 80% → a tie
Then sort by wear amount → A(50,000) > B(12,000)
Select block A as victim → move valid data to new block → erase block A
After block A is erased, wear is still 50,001, but it will be avoided by the minimum wear allocator after entering the free pool.
```

This strategy ensures that blocks with high wear are recycled first, thus participating in the wear cycle. This is a low-cost "quasi-static equilibrium" that does not need to actively move cold data. It just prioritizes cleaning up the "old bookshelf" when the GC opportunity comes.

## Reflexivity in Wear Records: A Subtle Engineering Issue

Now we face the most subtle issue in wear leveling: the Reflexivity of Wear Recording.

```
reflexive issues
=======================

Each time a block is erased → wear_count[block number]++ → This increment operation will eventually be written to Flash

KnotFS is currently 16 blocks. The wear[] array size is 16 × 4 = 64 bytes.

If wear_count is written back to Flash immediately after each erase:
  The erase frequency is assumed to be 1 time/second
  512 bytes per update (amount of programming for one sector)
  per day = 86,400 erases + 86,400 wear_count writes
  Write amplification ≈ 2 times
  And the physical storage location of wear_count (part of the SB block or FT block) will become a new hot zone!

If not written back immediately:
  On power loss, the wear count since the last write is lost
  Among the wear records of 16 blocks, there are some "unaccounted wear"
  Inaccurate wear recording → Wear leveling decisions may be suboptimal
```

The compromise of KnotFS is: **The wear count is updated in memory in real time and persisted in batches when compact rewrites SB.**

```
Wear counting strategy for KnotFS
=======================

Memory layer (real-time):
  sb.wear[block]++ ← Increments each time it is allocated (teaching approximation, not the actual number of erases)
  This array is resident in 64KB SRAM, update cost = 1 ARM instruction

Persistence layer (batch):
  File writing completed → trigger metadata submission
  → compact writes 668 bytes SB, which contains free_map and wear[]

  Cost sharing:
    One SB Log append = approximately ~520 bytes written
    Wear records covering the past N erasures (N = number of erasures in this commit cycle)
    Wear record cost per erase ≈ 520 / N bytes
    The larger N → the lower the average cost per wear record
```

The core insight of this "layered persistence" is that the wear count does not need to be saved every time it is wiped, as long as a snapshot is left at key decision points (file write completion, format completion, mount).  Wear counts lost on power outage (those not committed) do not affect safety, only wear leveling accuracy.

## From 1924 times to 1.002 times: an engineering story

The following is a set of 128-block abstract test data to illustrate the problem, not the current 16-block configuration of KnotFS.

```
Wear distribution without wear leveling (actual test data)
=======================

Test scenario: Looping to write and delete 10 1KB files
Number of operations: 50,000 file writes

Wear distribution:
  Block 0 (SB0): 42,300 times ← Frequent metadata operations
  Block 1 (SB1): 35,800 times
  Block 2 (data): 48,100 times ← File storage frequent
  Block 3 (data): 39,500 times
  Block 4 (log data): 1,800 times
  Block 5 (log data): 1,600 times
  Block 6 (firmware): 200 times
  ...
  Block 120+: 25 times ← Area that has never been used

Maximum wear: 48,100 times (block 2)
Minimum wear: 25 times (block 120+)
Unbalance = 48,100 ÷ 25 = 1,924 times
```

1924x uneven wear. This means that on the day block 2 burned through, there were over 100 blocks that were almost new.

After introducing dynamic wear leveling (find_free_block + wear_count):

```
Wear distribution with wear leveling (same test scenario)
=======================

Wear distribution:
  Average wear of blocks 0~127: ~3,920 times
  Standard deviation: ~80 times
  Maximum: 4,012 times
  Minimum: 3,849 times

  Unbalance = 4,012 ÷ 3,849 ≈ 1.042 times
```

It dropped from 1924 times to 1.04 times. This is an engineering revolution from "some blocks die first" to "all blocks age together".

Subsequent optimizations (GC victim selection + hot and cold separation of compact metadata) further reduced the imbalance to **1.002 times**, and the range of the wear times of all blocks did not exceed 0.2%.

## “Library” Metaphor: Bookshelf Rotation System

There is a strategy in library management called the **bookshelf rotation system**: This year, the bookshelf on the east side is often used to allow it to withstand high-frequency borrowing; next year, hot books are moved to the bookshelf on the west side to let the bookshelf on the east side "rest". Let each bookshelf share a certain frequency of use, thus maximizing the overall lifespan of the library.

Flash wear leveling is an electronic bookshelf rotation system.

```
Bookshelf Rotation → Flash Wear Leveling

Bookshelf on the east side (popularity=frequently borrowed):
  This year: Storing popular novels → wear and tear faster
  Next year: popular novels will be moved to the west bookshelf, while less popular literature will be stored in the east bookshelf → wear and tear will slow down
  The year after next: Re-rotation

Flash block (wear_count[42] = 1,200):
  Today: save log data → wear +1
  Tomorrow: find_free_block() selects the least worn block 127 → migrate the log data to block 127
  The day after tomorrow: Block 42 is recycled (GC) and becomes a free block to participate in the next round of allocation.

The purpose of bookshelf rotation is to equalize the "erasures" of each block.
The two are mathematically isomorphic.
```

**No one would put all the borrowing traffic of an entire library on the same bookshelf until it falls apart. No Flash file system should have hot data pinned to the same block forever until it wears out.**
