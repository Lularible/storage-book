# 1.1 Knot Records: Humanity's First Storage Record

## You have a sentence in your mind, but you keep your mouth shut

Picture this: you are walking in the Stone Age wilderness with a hemp rope tied around your waist. As you walk, you discover a herd of bison three mountains to the east. You need to tell the hunters in the tribe this news.

You can run back. You can shout. But what if the hunters aren't around when you arrive, and you have to continue scouting in another direction?

You squat down and tie a knot on the hemp rope.

This is not a casual knot. The position, size and winding method of this knot all represent a message: "To the east, there are many cows."

You get up and leave, leaving behind the rope tied to the tree. Three days later, the hunter passed by, saw the knot, understood the message, and headed east.

**Knot records are the first "storage system" in human history.**

It is not storage because there are no heads, no floating gates, and no charges. But it has completed the core function of storage: **information leaves you, but it still exists**.

That knot is the first line of human code. It abstracts information from time so that it does not need to rely on specific people, specific places, and specific moments. You are gone, but the information is still there.

---

## The "agreement" in the knot

Recording events with knots is not an act for one's own entertainment. It is a set of protocols.

Modern archaeological and anthropological research has found that knotted rope note-taking systems in various places have surprisingly similar structures:

```
Knot protocol stack (ancient version)
========================

Application layer: "There are cows in the east" ← Specific information to be transmitted
Expression layer: Major knot=animal, minor knot=quantity ← Symbol convention
Encoding layer: Knot position + size + color ← Physical representation
Physical layer: hemp rope, straw rope, palm fiber ← storage medium
```

This is not a casual knot. It is a complete coding system. The size of the knot represents the importance of the information, the position of the knot represents the type of information, and the color of the rope represents the field of information (for example, red rope records war, yellow records food...).

The most famous example is the Inca Empire's Quipu, a system of recording national information using knotted ropes. The Inca Empire had no writing, but they used quipu to record censuses, taxes, grain reserves, and troop movements. A skilled khipu reader can "read" hundreds of knot records in an hour.

The structure of Khipu is extremely complex:

```
Main rope (horizontal)
 ├── The first hanging rope → [●●●] [●●] [●] ← Three large knots represent 300, two small knots represent 20, and one small knot represents 1
 │ = 321 (some kind of unit)
 ├── Second hanging rope → Empty rope ← Placeholder to separate different items
 ├── The third hanging rope → [●●●●] [●●●●] ← Data
 │ └── Sub-sling → [●][●][●] ← Sub-entry, may be remarks
 └── The fourth hanging rope → different colors ← different categories
```

What does this have to do with "tying a few knots"? This is a complete database implemented using the rope topology.

The Incas even used khipus to implement a concept similar to "checksum": at the end of the knot system, there is a special "checksum knot", and the reader needs to check whether the previous data is consistent with this checksum knot. If they are inconsistent, it means that the rope may have become loose during transportation, or the interpretation may have been incorrect.

**Thousands of years ago in the Andes Mountains, people were already using "CRC". **

## Three core issues of storage

Knot records may seem primitive, but it reveals three core issues in storage technology, and these three issues have never changed from knots to Flash.

**The first question: encoding. ** You have an idea in your head, how do you turn it into something in the physical world?

Knot's answer is: convention symbology. "The big knot represents hundreds, the small knot represents ten, and the single circle represents one." This set of agreements must be established in advance between the sender and the receiver. Without common coding rules, knotting notes is just a ball of hemp rope.

This is not fundamentally different from Flash storage. You write a file to Flash, and the process is to program the byte sequence to the threshold voltage of the floating gate transistor. This is also a kind of encoding: turning logical data into a physical state. What are the encoding rules? It is the metadata structure of the file system (superblock, inode, FAT table...).

**Second question: durability. ** How long is the message retained after it leaves the sender?

Twine can last for hundreds of years in the dry climate of the Andes. Clay tablet inscriptions can be preserved for thousands of years, and archaeologists today can still decipher Sumerian cuneiform writing. Paper can last thousands of years under ideal conditions. However, magnetic core memory is forgotten when the power is turned off. DRAM needs to be refreshed every few milliseconds. Although Flash is non-volatile, it will wear out if written too much.

Every storage medium has its "life script". The length of this script determines the validity period of the information.**Third question: Searchability.** The information is saved; how can I retrieve it?

You can't touch a hundred ropes one by one, you need a concept of "catalogue". Inca Khipu's interpreters have received rigorous training. They know which rope records what content, and know which node on the main rope to start "reading" from.

This is the same as the essence of the file system's directory structure, inode index, and FAT linked list. They are all "metadata" and "information about information."

## From knots to clay tablets: the first leap in storage

From knotted ropes to clay tablets, it is a qualitative leap in human storage technology. The leap is the abstraction level, not the hardware itself.

Knots can only record numerical information, such as quantity, amount, and date. It hardly conveys the narrative "Today the king shot a deer while hunting." But clay tablets do. Cuneiform was able to express the entire vocabulary and grammar of Sumerian in less than a thousand symbols.

Here is a very profound observation: **The evolution of storage media is essentially the evolution of the abstraction capabilities of data models. **

```
Knot → numerical code → quantity, date
Clay Tablet → Text Encoding → Narrative, Law, Literature
Paper → Large-scale reproduction → Printing industrializes information
Disk → File system abstraction → You no longer care which sector the data is in
Flash → Flash Translation Layer (FTL) → You don’t even care about the physical properties of the media
```

Each level of abstraction takes you one step further away from the physical medium. At the same time, each layer of abstraction **covers** the defects of the physical media, including wear, bad blocks, and power outages, allowing upper-level users to see a "perfect" storage device.

And this cover-up is the core of what our book is about.

---

## The ancestor of the book in your hand

When you write a line of code using `knotfs_write("hello.txt", "Hello World", 11)`, your computer encodes, stores, and indexes the information in less than a millisecond.

You don’t need to care about which physical block the data exists in (find_free_block selects the youngest brick for you), you don’t need to care about whether the writing process will be powered off midway (Copy-on-Write protects old data for you), and you don’t need to care about how the metadata is safely landed (log-structured metadata adds an 8-byte record to the SB Log for you).

Behind these "no need to care" is an engineering relay that has lasted for tens of thousands of years. Starting with the knot-tyers, generations of engineers have been solving the same problem: making information outlive us. **

The person tying the knot may not know that the "program" of the hemp rope in his hand will run for tens of thousands of years, and will finally be compiled into C code in 2026 and run in a 64KB embedded file system.

But the things he does and the things we do, the soul is the same.

**The essence of storage is to resist the entropy increase of time. Data naturally tends to be chaotic in the physical world, such as charge leakage, write interruption, block wear, bit flipping... and the file system is the engineering crystallization of human wisdom to combat this trend. **
