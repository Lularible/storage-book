# 1.1 Knot Records: Humanity's First Storage Record

## You have a sentence in your mind, but you keep your mouth shut

Picture this: you are walking through Stone Age wilderness with a hemp rope tied around your waist. You discover a herd of bison three mountains to the east. You need to get the news back to the hunters in your tribe.

You could run back. You could shout. But what if the hunters are away when you arrive, and you still have to keep scouting in another direction?

So you squat down and tie a knot in the rope.

This is not just any knot. Its position, size, and pattern of twists all carry a message: "To the east, many bison."

You leave the rope tied to a tree. Three days later, a hunter passes by, sees the knot, understands the message, and heads east.

**Knot records are the first "storage system" in human history.**

They have no read head, no floating gate, no electric charge. Yet they perform the core function of storage: **information can leave you and still survive.**

That knot is the first line of human code. It lifts information out of the moment, so the message no longer depends on a particular person, place, or time. You are gone; the information is still there.

---

## The "protocol" inside a knot

Recording events with knots was not a private hobby. It was a protocol.

Modern archaeology and anthropology have found that knot-record systems in different cultures share a surprisingly similar structure:

```
Knot protocol stack (ancient version)
========================

Application layer: "There are bison in the east" ← the message to transmit
Expression layer: large knot = animal, small knot = quantity ← symbol convention
Encoding layer: knot position + size + color ← physical representation
Physical layer: hemp rope, straw rope, palm fiber ← storage medium
```

The size of a knot can mark importance. Its position can mark the type of information. The color of the rope can mark the category: red for war, yellow for grain, and so on.

The most famous example is the Inca *khipu*, a system for recording state information with knotted cords. The Inca had no writing, but they used khipu to record censuses, taxes, grain reserves, and troop movements. A skilled khipu reader could "read" hundreds of records in an hour.

The structure of a khipu is remarkably complex:

```
Main cord (horizontal)
 ├── First pendant cord → [●●●] [●●] [●] ← three large knots = 300, two small = 20, one = 1
 │ = 321 (in some unit)
 ├── Second pendant cord → empty cord ← placeholder separating entries
 ├── Third pendant cord → [●●●●] [●●●●] ← data
 │ └── subsidiary cord → [●][●][●] ← sub-entry, possibly a note
 └── Fourth pendant cord → different color ← a different category
```

This is not "tying a few knots." It is a database built out of rope topology.

The Inca even used something like a checksum: a special "checksum knot" at the end of a record. The reader compares the earlier data with that knot. If they do not match, the rope may have loosened in transit, or the reading may be wrong.

**Thousands of years ago in the Andes, people were already using something like a CRC.**

---

## Three core problems of storage

Knot records look primitive, but they reveal three problems that every storage technology still faces.

**The first problem: encoding.** You have an idea in your head. How do you turn it into something in the physical world?

The answer was a shared convention: a large knot means hundreds, a small knot means tens, a single loop means one. Sender and receiver must agree on the code in advance. Without that agreement, knot records are just a ball of hemp rope.

Flash storage is not fundamentally different. Writing a file means programming a sequence of bytes into the threshold voltages of floating-gate transistors. That is also encoding: logical data becomes a physical state. The encoding rules live in the file-system metadata: superblocks, inodes, FAT tables, and so on.

**The second problem: durability.** How long does the message survive after it leaves the sender?

Twine can last for centuries in the dry Andes. Clay tablets can survive for millennia; archaeologists still decipher Sumerian cuneiform today. Paper can last thousands of years under ideal conditions. Magnetic-core memory, by contrast, forgets when the power goes off. DRAM must be refreshed every few milliseconds. Flash is non-volatile, but it wears out after too many writes.

Every medium has a lifetime script. That script determines how long the information remains valid.

**The third problem: searchability.** Once information is stored, how do you find it again?

You cannot inspect a hundred ropes one by one. You need a catalogue. Inca khipu readers underwent rigorous training. They knew which cord held which kind of record, and where on the main cord to start reading.

This is the same problem that directory trees, inode indexes, and FAT chains solve. They are all metadata: information about information.

---

## From knots to clay tablets: the first leap

The move from knotted cords to clay tablets was a qualitative leap, but the leap was in abstraction, not in hardware.

Knots mainly record quantities: how many, how much, on what date. They are poor at narrative, such as "Today the king shot a deer while hunting." Clay tablets can do that. Cuneiform could express the vocabulary and grammar of Sumerian in fewer than a thousand symbols.

The deeper observation is this: **the evolution of storage media is largely the evolution of data models.**

```
Knot → numerical code → quantity, date
Clay tablet → text encoding → narrative, law, literature
Paper → mass reproduction → printing industrializes information
Disk → file-system abstraction → you no longer care which sector holds the data
Flash → Flash Translation Layer (FTL) → you no longer care about the media's physical quirks
```

Each layer of abstraction moves you further from the physical medium. Each layer also hides the medium's defects, including wear, bad blocks, and power loss, so the layer above sees a "perfect" storage device.

That act of hiding is what this book is about.

---

## The ancestor of the book in your hand

When you call `knotfs_write("hello.txt", "Hello World", 11)`, your computer encodes, stores, and indexes the information in less than a millisecond.

You do not need to know which physical block holds the data; `find_free_block` picks the youngest brick for you. You do not need to worry about a power loss in the middle of the write; Copy-on-Write protects the old data. You do not need to know how metadata reaches Flash safely; the log-structured metadata path appends an 8-byte record to the SB Log.

Behind all this "you do not need to care" is an engineering relay tens of thousands of years long. It began with the knot-tyers. Generation after generation of engineers has attacked the same problem: how to make information outlive us.

The person tying that knot could not know that the "program" in their hands would keep running for millennia, and would eventually be compiled into C and run inside a 64KB embedded file system in 2026.

But the work is the same. The soul is the same.

**The essence of storage is to resist the entropy of time. Data naturally drifts toward disorder in the physical world: charge leaks, writes are interrupted, blocks wear out, bits flip. A file system is the engineering crystallization of our attempt to push back.**
