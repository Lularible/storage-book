# 2.4 The Three Original Sins of Flash: Erase-Before-Write, One-Way Programming, Finite Endurance

## You are an electron trapped in a floating gate

Imagine a scenario: you are not writing code. You are an electron, forced into the floating gate of a Flash memory cell by the Fowler-Nordheim tunneling effect.

You are surrounded by 10 nanometers of silicon dioxide. You can't get out of this wall. The only purpose of you being locked here is to tell the sense amplifier outside a message: "1" or "0". Specifically, if you and your companions have enough (about a few thousand electrons), the threshold voltage of the floating gate is pulled high, and the sense amplifier determines that this is "0". If there are few of them and the threshold voltage is low, it is judged as "1".

You are "0".

Would you like to go out? You thought the wipe operation would get you out?

Yes. Erasure is the only way out. When the erase pulse comes, a strong electric field of about 20V is applied to both ends of the oxide layer in the reverse direction. You and your companions will return to the substrate through the F-N tunneling effect. The floating gate is empty, the threshold voltage drops, and "0" changes back to "1".

But there's a key limitation: **You can only wait for someone to open the door. You can't go out on your own. And whoever opens the door must open the entire cell block as well.**

This is Flash's first sin.

---

## Original Sin 1: Erase-Before-Write

On disk, modifying a byte is modifying a byte. The magnetic head flies to the target sector, flips a magnetic domain, and it's done. New data overwrites old data and is updated in place.

On Flash, it is impossible to modify one byte.

Why?

Because the physical programming operation of Flash can only do one thing: **turn "1" into "0"**. More precisely, electrons are injected into the floating gate to raise the threshold voltage. The reverse operation, that is, changing "0" back to "1", requires erasing. And erasure is not in bytes. The granularity of erasure is a sector (sector, 4KB) or a block (block, 64KB or even 256KB).

what does that mean? Let's look at a specific scenario.

Suppose you have a 64KB NOR Flash block that contains a piece of firmware calibration data. You only need to modify 4 bytes (one calibration parameter). What is your train of thought?

```
Naive thinking (disk thinking – wrong!)
=======================

Before modification: [ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] 64KB data
                ↑
          To change these 4 bytes

You want: [ABCDEFGH####MNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] to directly overwrite 4 bytes
                ↑
          Write new data

Reality: ✗ Can’t do it.
         Flash programming can only change 1→0, not 0→1.
         In these 4 bytes, some bits need to change from 0→1,
         You can't.
```

You can only erase the entire 64KB block, turn it all to "1"s, and then reprogram it:

```
Flash’s forced approach (Copy-on-Write)
=======================

Step 1: [ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] Read the entire block into the RAM buffer

Step 2: [ABCDEFGH####MNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] Modify those 4 bytes in the buffer

Step 3: [███████████████████████████████] ← Erase the entire 64KB block
         ﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿
         ﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿
         All become 0xFF 0xFF 0xFF...

Step 4: [ABCDEFGH####MNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] Program the buffer data back to Flash
                                        ← Takes ~a few milliseconds
Total time: ~200ms + read time + write time
```

**To modify 4 bytes, you erased 65536 bytes, and then rewritten 65536 bytes. The efficiency is 4/65536 = 0.006%.**

This is determined by the physical design of Flash. The erase granularity of NAND is larger, usually one block (several MB). The absolute value of erasure time is smaller (~3ms), but the granularity is larger. No matter what kind of Flash, this problem cannot be avoided.

So the Flash file system must adopt a completely different strategy: **Out-of-Place Update**.

```
Offsite update strategy
=======================

Old location: [ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] ← Marked as "junk"
         Rubbish rubbish rubbish rubbish...

New location: [ABCDEFGH####MNOPQRSTUVWXYZ...] ← Write new data to a
                                             Free erased blocks
        ↑
   Only these 4 bytes are different

The old location is not erased immediately. It's just marked as "invalid".
After accumulating enough garbage,
Garbage Collection erases them in batches.
```

This means: **Flash file system does not have the concept of "in-place modification".** Every "modify file" operation you see is a new write at the physical layer. Old data becomes garbage, waiting to be recycled.

Is it like when you are writing a paper, you have to copy the entire paper from scratch every time you revise it, correct the parts that need to be changed, and then throw the old version into the wastebasket?

This is the philosophy of Copy-on-Write. It is the only solution forced by Flash file system design.

## Original Sin 2: One-way Programming

Let's take a closer look at the physics of Flash programming.

Programming a Flash unit is doing this operation: **1 → 0**. You add electrons to the floating gate.

Erasing a Flash unit is doing this operation: **0 → 1**. You remove the electrons from the floating gate.

The operations in these two directions have completely different mechanisms:

| Operation | Direction | Mechanism | Voltage | Granularity |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| Programming | 1→0 | CHE (Channel Hot Electron Injection) or F-N Tunneling | ~10-20V pulses | Bytes/page |
| Erase | 0→1 | F-N tunneling (reverse) | ~20V | Sector/Block |

NOR Flash programming uses channel hot electron injection (CHE), which is fast but has high power consumption; NAND Flash programming uses F-N tunneling, which has low power consumption but slow speed.
Pay attention to the "Granularity" column. The smallest unit of programming is usually a byte (NOR) or a page (NAND, usually 2KB-16KB). The smallest unit of erasure is a sector (4KB-64KB) or a block (tens of KB to several MB).

**The granularity of erase and program is asymmetric. This is the root of the whole problem.**

You can program byte by byte in an erased block. no problem. But if one of the bytes has "0" written on it and you want to change it to "1", sorry, you have to erase the whole block.

```
Program vs Erase Granular Asymmetry
=======================

Programming granularity (NOR): █ ← 1 byte
Programming Granularity (NAND): ████████████████ ← 1 page (2KB~16KB)

Erase granularity (NOR): █████████████████████████████████████████████
                    ← 1 sector (4KB~64KB)

Erase granularity (NAND): █████████████████████████████████████████████
                    ﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿
                    ﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿﻿
                    ← 1 block (usually 64~256 pages, or a few MB)


                        Programming granularity Erase granularity Asymmetric scaling
        NOR 1B 4KB ~ 64KB 4096:1 ~ 65536:1
        NAND 2KB ~ 16KB 64 pages ~ 256 pages 64:1 ~ 256:1
```

This asymmetric ratio directly determines the data structure design of the Flash file system. You can't use a linked list to string sectors together like FAT32, because "modifying a FAT entry" means erasing and rewriting. You must also make metadata log-structured and append changes to the end instead of overwriting them in place.

Flash’s original sin of “erase before writing” has given rise to two completely different technical routes in the design of Flash file systems:

1. FTL (Flash Translation Layer) route: Add a layer of logical-physical mapping on top of Flash to make the upper file system (such as FAT32, ext4) think that it can modify it in place. In fact, FTL performs off-site updates behind the scenes. The price is the memory overhead of the mapping table and write amplification.

2. Native Flash file system route: The file system itself senses the physical characteristics of Flash and directly builds a log-structured data structure on it. KnotFS, which will be discussed later, takes this path. Its SB Log and all data blocks are only appended and not overwritten. Each "modification" is a new write.

Both paths are responses to "in-place modification is impossible", but the division of labor is different: FTL hides the complexity in the driver layer, and the native file system exposes the complexity in the file system layer.

## Original Sin III: Limited Lifespan

Flash will die.

Not a metaphor. It will die. Each erasure operation physically wears away the tunnel oxide layer.

To erase, you use a strong electric field (~20V) to pull electrons out of the floating gate. The process of electrons passing through the oxide layer will cause traps, defect states at the oxide-silicon interface. Every trap is a new leakage channel. The more traps there are, the faster electrons will escape. The electrons escape faster and the threshold voltage drifts. If the threshold voltage drifts, the reading result will be wrong.

This is an irreversible degradation process.

```
Flash cell life degradation process
=======================

P/E = 0 (new):
  ┌─────────────────┐
  │ Floating gate ██████████ │ The oxide layer is smooth and complete
  │ ████████████████│ ← The electrons stay in the floating gate
  │ ████████████████│ Almost no leaks
  └─────────────────┘

P/E = 10,000 (NOR 10% life):
  ┌─────────────────┐
  │ Floating gate ██████████ │ The oxide layer begins to trap
  │ █████X████X██████│ ← X = trap, electrons leak from here
  │ ██████X██████████│ The leak rate begins to increase
  └─────────────────┘

P/E = 100,000 (NOR life limit):
  ┌─────────────────┐
  │ Floating gate ██████████ │ The oxide layer is full of traps
  │ ████X████X██X██ │ ← Electronic leakage out of control
  │
  └─────────────────┘
  Unit performance:
    - Programming time becomes longer (stronger pulses are required to reach the target voltage)
    - Erase time becomes longer (oxidation layer degradation causes F-N tunneling efficiency to decrease)
    - Data retention time drops (electrons leak quickly from the trap)
    - Eventually: cannot be programmed or cannot be erased → bad block
```

For NOR Flash, this life limit is approximately **100,000 P/E cycles**. For consumer-grade TLC NAND, it's about **1,000 to 3,000 times**.

100,000 times sounds like a lot, right? Let's do some math.

Let's say you have a block of NOR Flash and your application erases it every hour:
```
100,000 times ÷ 24 hours ÷ 365 days ≈ 11.4 years
```

Looks fine. But the problem is **uneven wear**.

All your file operations are not evenly distributed across all blocks. Imagine a scenario: your system has a small configuration file (for example, a few hundred bytes), and the timestamp is updated every time the system starts. This file may only occupy one sector. If the system erases the same sector every time it starts...

```
10 starts a day × 365 days = 3,650 times/year
100,000 times ÷ 3,650 times/year ≈ 27 years
```

Looks ok. But if it is a car application...

```
One engine ignition = one start
100 starts per day × 365 days = 36,500 times/year (more extreme assumption)
100,000 ÷ 36,500 ≈ 2.7 years
```

**A NOR Flash block specially used to store the "system startup timestamp" has a lifespan of less than 3 years on the car.**

This is why you need **Wear Leveling**.

The core idea of ​​wear leveling is simple: don't keep hot data pinned to the same piece.  Select a free block with the least number of wears for each write. This requires global tracking of the number of erases for all blocks.

```
Wear leveling indication
=======================

No wear leveling:
  Block 0: 99999 times (almost dead)
  Block 1: █ 1 times
  Block 2: ██ 2 times
  ...
  Block 127: 0 times (not used at all)

  Problem: Block 0 dies prematurely

With wear leveling:
  Block 0: ████ 800 times
  Block 1: ████ 790 times
  Block 2: ████ 810 times
  ...
  Block 127: ████ 795 times

  All blocks age evenly, maximizing overall lifespan
```

This brings us back to the wear count array and free block selection function in the file system. They are the basic survival strategies of the Flash file system.

## The First Sins

In addition to these three original sins, Flash also has several "sub-original sins". They do not determine the fundamental architecture of the file system like the first three, but they must also be dealt with in engineering practice.

**First Sin: Read Disturb**

We already talked about it in the previous section. Repeatedly reading cells at one location causes slight soft programming on adjacent word lines. This is especially serious for NAND's planar architecture, since all cells in the same block share word lines. The solution is: track the number of reads for each block, migrate the data to new blocks when the threshold is exceeded, and erase the old blocks.

**Second Original Sin: Program Disturb**

When you program a cell, you apply a high voltage to the word line. Other cells on the same word line that do not need to be programmed will also feel this high voltage. They may be "accidentally programmed", with the threshold voltage moving slightly upward, and the "1" in the erased state slowly drifting toward the "0" in the programmed state. The manufacturer's solution is to use self-boosted program inhibition technology (Self-Boosted Program Inhibit) in the programming algorithm to apply a self-boosted electric field on the cell channel that does not require programming to offset the programming voltage.

However, the project still requires additional protection from the file system: verification after writing data, recording error locations, and avoiding repeated partial programming of the same word line.

**The Third Original Sin: Data Retention**

We have discussed charge leakage in detail. Here is a piece of trivia: **Data retention depends not only on temperature, but also on the time since the last erasure.** A Flash chip has the best data retention when it leaves the factory (P/E=0). After 100,000 erases (P/E=100,000), the data retention capacity is only about one-tenth of what it was when it left the factory.

This means: **Later in the chip's life cycle, your ECC must be stronger.** The computational load of LDPC soft decision decoding increases significantly when the chip is old, which is one of the reasons why the reading speed of old SSD is slower than that of new SSD.

## Compared with disk: another philosophy of Flash design

Let's compare Flash and disk side by side, and you'll see the full motivation behind the design of the Flash file system.

```
Flash vs Disk: A Comparative Framework
=======================

                       Disk HDD NOR Flash NAND Flash
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Read granularity 512B/4KB byte page (2KB-16KB)
Write granularity 512B/4KB byte (1→0) page (2KB-16KB)
Erase granularity None (direct overwrite) Sector (4KB-64KB) Block (tens of KB~several MB)
Erase time Not required 50ms-200ms 1ms-5ms
Modify in place ✓ ✗ ✗
P/E life unlimited (theoretically) 100,000 500-100,000
Random read latency 5-10ms (seek) 70-100ns ~25μs
Random write latency 5-10ms (seek) Erase + Program, higher latency GC required, latency extremely uncertain
Fragmentation effects Increased seek times None (random access flat) None
Whether wear leveling is required depends on the writing mode Required Required
Whether garbage collection is required Usually not required (native NOR) File system layer may be required Required
Whether ECC is required? Sector CRC/ECC is usually used. Simple ECC is enough. BCH/LDPC strong ECC.
```

There is only one issue to consider with disks: seek time.  Flash needs to consider erase granularity, programming directionality, P/E wear, read interference, programming interference, data retention...

The core question of a disk file system is: "In which sectors should the data be placed to reduce seeks?"

The core question of the Flash file system is: "Which blocks should the data be placed in to achieve even wear, minimize garbage, save the most GC, and be the safest when powered off?"

**Completely two philosophies.**

The speed bottleneck of disks is mechanical motion. The speed bottleneck of Flash is physical degradation.

Disk reliability relies on physical redundancy (RAID). The reliability of Flash relies on mathematical redundancy (ECC).

The disk will break, usually because the head hits the disk or the motor fails, which is intuitive and physical. Flash will break because electrons slowly leak from the 10-nanometer-wide oxide layer, which is microscopic, quantum, and invisible. Once you understand these differences, you will understand why porting a file system from disk to Flash (such as FAT32 running directly on NAND) is a disaster, because it assumes "can be modified in place" and "no wear and tear", both of which are wrong on Flash.

```
If FAT32 runs directly on Flash (nightmare scenario)
=======================

The FAT table of FAT32 is a hot data area that is frequently modified.
Every time a file is written, the FAT table entry must be updated.

On disk:
  FAT table entry update → Move the magnetic head to the FAT area → Overwrite and write → Complete
  Cost: One seek

On Flash:
  FAT table entry update → Erase the entire sector where the FAT is located → Rewrite the entire sector
  Cost: Erase Once + Write Amplification × Sector Size

If 10 FAT updates occur every second:
  Disk: 10 seeks
  Flash: 10 erases, burning one sector per second
  100,000 ÷ 864,000 ≈ 0.116 days ≈ 2.8 hours; life will be exhausted in less than 3 hours
```

This is why FTL (Flash Translation Layer) exists. It inserts a layer of logical-physical mapping between FAT32 and Flash, making FAT32 think it is "modifying in place". In fact, FTL does Copy-on-Write and garbage collection behind the scenes.

The file system you designed is a file system that "knows that it is on Flash". It doesn't need FTL because it was born for Flash's three original sins.

## Write and enlarge: original sin under original sin

The three original sins joined forces to produce a more destructive secondary effect: **Write Amplification**.

The definition of write amplification is simple:

```
Write amplification factor = number of bytes actually written to Flash ÷ number of bytes requested to be written by the file system
```

In an ideal world (the ideal world of disks), write amplification factor = 1. If you write 1KB of data, the disk will write 1KB. But Flash is not an ideal world.

Let’s look at a specific TLC NAND SSD scenario:

```
How a small write is amplified in the "page mapping FTL + GC" scenario
=======================

Application layer: write("log.txt", 512 bytes)
  ↓
File system: Find the logical page where the file is located
  ↓
FTL: This logical page maps to a page of physical block A
     ← But the smallest programming unit of NAND is the page (16KB)!
     ← You can’t just write 512 bytes onto a page
  Therefore:
      The normal path of page mapping FTL: just allocate a new physical page, write new data, update logical page → physical page mapping, old page mark is invalid.
      The following whole block copy belongs to GC/block recycling, which is the worst case, not lowercase every time:
      1. Read other valid pages in physical block A
      2. Allocate a new free block B
      3. Move the valid pages to block B
      4. Update mapping table
      5. Erase block A

  Normal path write volume: one new page (about 16KB); about 32 times the write amplification of 512B.
  If GC is triggered, the effective page relocation amount will also be added, and the write amplification will be higher.
```

This is just one operation. If many writes continue, GC garbage collection will further amplify the write amplification:

```
Secondary amplification effect of GC
=======================

Scenario: One NAND block has 128 pages
  Among them: 100 pages are valid data and 28 pages are garbage (marked as invalid)
  Insufficient free space → trigger GC

GC process:
  1. Select the victim block - select the block with the most garbage
  2. Read 100 pages of valid data in the victim block → write to another free block
  3. Erase the victim block → it now becomes a free block

  Actual erase: 1 block (128 pages)
  Actual writing: 100 pages
  ─────────────────────
  The GC itself causes additional wear and tear on the storage media.
  On an almost full SSD, write amplification with sustained writes can
  Easily over 10x.
```

**Write amplification is the core indicator to measure the quality of a Flash file system design.** A good Flash file system, its GC strategy, hot and cold data separation, and write batch aggregation, all are designed to point to the same goal: to keep the write amplification factor as close to 1 as possible.

In your implementation, the indirect block exists to reduce write amplification: only the modified pointer entries in the indirect block are modified, rather than the entire file data being rewritten. This is a miniature implementation of the original "separation of hot and cold" idea.

## Why does NAND have bad blocks, but NOR has almost none?

You may have noticed a phenomenon: the NAND Flash data sheet clearly states that "no more than 2% of the initial bad blocks are allowed", while the NOR Flash data sheet has almost no bad block specifications.

why is that?

The answer lies in the combination of these three original sins.

To extremely reduce costs, the NAND manufacturing process has compressed the size of each unit to the limit (15nm-20nm process node). At this size, the number of dies on a 300mm wafer is staggering, but the defect probability of each die is also higher. If a nanometer-sized particle falls on the oxide layer, the unit will be bad.

NOR unit is about 10F², NAND is about 4F², NOR is about 2.5 times that of NAND; the specific ratio changes with the process. Larger area means lower defect density. At the same time, NOR's more conservative process nodes (usually 65nm-45nm) also mean higher yields.

The deeper reason lies in the architectural differences between NOR and NAND:

- NOR erasure is uniform, the entire sector is erased at the same time, and the threshold voltage distribution of each cell after erasure is very neat.
- NAND is erased block by block, but the bit line and word line architecture inside the block causes the erase speed of edge cells and central cells to be inconsistent. Some units are erased slowly (under-erase), and some are erased quickly (over-erase). The voltage distribution after erasure is wider than NOR, and some cells may be inherently substandard.

Therefore, NAND allows up to 2% (some specifications are 1%) of initial bad blocks when shipped from the factory. These bad blocks are discovered during factory probe testing, recorded, and stored in a reserved area of ​​the chip (Initial Bad Block Mark). The Flash file system must read this bad block table during initialization and exclude them from the available block pool. As for NOR, in the same probe test, the bad block rate is usually less than a few ten thousandths. This is another physical basis for NOR's "reliable" image.

## Project adaptation list of Flash Original Sin

The three original sins of Flash determine the data structure of every Flash file system. Check out these coping strategies:

```
Original Sin of Flash → Engineering Response
=======================

Erase before writing → Out-of-Place Update (out-of-place update, no in-place overwrite)
One-way programming → Copy-on-Write (all "modify" operations are new)
Limited life → wear leveling + bad block management
Read interference → read count + migrate data when threshold is exceeded
```

These strategies are the basic survival conditions of the Flash file system. You will see later that every deliberately designed detail of KnotFS, that is, the additional writing of SB Log, the data block of Copy-on-Write, and the global tracking of the wear count array, are the direct products of these original sins.

**Understanding the original sin of Flash is a necessary and sufficient condition for understanding the design of Flash file system.**

## Counter common sense: the "original sin" of original sin

**Flash's three original sins, the real "original sin" may be the disk.**

The reason why it is said to be "counterintuitive" is because we have always used the convenience of disks to judge the shortcomings of Flash. But look at it from another perspective:

If you are a database engineer and deal with PostgreSQL's WAL (Write-Ahead Log) every day, you will feel that "off-site update" and "append writing" are natural. This is exactly the transaction log mechanism of the database.

The three original sins of Flash actually force the file system to adopt a data management strategy that is almost the same as the database design:

- Database's WAL = Flash file system's log-structured metadata
- MVCC (Multiple Version Concurrency Control) of database = Copy-on-Write of Flash file system
- VACUUM/compression of database = garbage collection of Flash file system
- Database block wear (SSD life) = Flash file system wear leveling

**The database is not "bad", it assumes from the beginning that the physical media is unreliable, so logs and transactions are used to protect the data. The disk gives you the opposite education: you can modify it in place, you don't have to worry about wear and tear, and physics is transparent and reliable.**

Therefore, the three original sins of Flash, namely erase before writing, one-way programming, and limited life, are actually the true face of physical media. The disk just hides this appearance: its magnetic head can fly to any sector and directly overwrite it, its magnetic media life is almost unlimited, its bad sectors are transparently remapped by the controller, and the "perfect block device" you see is an illusion created by the disk's mechanical precision and redundant sectors.

**Flash's Three Original Sins tears away this illusion. The truth it tells you is: physical media are never perfect. The disk just hides its imperfections deeper.**

This may be the reason why I chose to start from Flash and start from the bottom in this book: what I am really learning is "how to build reliable abstractions in the unreliable physical world."

And this learning, starting from your understanding of the three original sins of Flash, has already completed the most important first step.
