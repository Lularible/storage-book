# 1.4 The Original Sin of Storage: Uncertainty in the Physical World

## You are not a god, you live in the physical world

Imagine a scenario. You are God. You create "data" in the void, a well-ordered set of zeros and ones, perfect, flawless, and eternal. You store it in the void, where there is no passage of time, no laws of physics, no entropy, and the data is always clean and unchanged.

But this scenario does not exist.

You are not God. You are a mortal engineer, and all you can command is the sand refined by human industrial civilization on a small piece of silicon wafer. You want to write a file on your Flash chip, and what is ultimately implemented in each storage unit is a specific number of electrons trapped in a very small floating gate, rather than abstract "zeroes and ones".

Electrons are very small. The number of electrons in the floating gate ranges from hundreds to thousands. Yes, the "signal" of each memory cell is distinguished by the presence or absence of hundreds of electrons. The smaller the transistor, the fewer electrons involved in characterizing the signal. When the information of a memory unit is determined by a quantitative difference of less than a hundred electrons, you are no longer operating a "digital circuit", you are operating "statistical mechanics".

A random thermal movement of an electron can flip your bit.

And this is not a bug. This is the nature of the physical world. Entropy is the only direction of the universe, and all ordered states will spontaneously evolve toward disorder. **Storage is essentially an attempt to maintain a small island of order in the tide of increasing entropy. But this island is surrounded by water and has undercurrents. Every second, various forces in the physical world are trying to destroy your island. **

This is the "original sin" of storage. These are three fundamental contradictions that all information preservation systems that rely on the physical world must face.

## Original Sin 1: Wear-Writing is damaging the medium

Let's start with the most intuitive fact: **Every time you write to Flash, you are physically destroying this storage unit. **

Answer a question first: Why does Flash have an upper limit on the number of erases?

Because each erase operation requires an extremely high electric field (about 10 MV/cm) to be applied at both ends of the tunnel oxide layer, forcing the electrons in the floating gate to tunnel through this oxide layer through Fowler-Nordheim tunneling. The process of electrons passing through the oxide layer is not free, it will cause microscopic **trap states** in the oxide layer.

```
Wear mechanism of Flash tunnel oxide layer

Before erasing:
   ┌─────────┐
   │ Floating Gate │ Electronics ↑
   ├─────────┤
   │ SiO₂ │ ← Perfect insulation layer, no defects
   ├─────────┤
   │Substrate │
   └─────────┘

Erase once:
   High-energy electrons ──→ crash through SiO₂ ──→ leaving microscopic defects (trap states) in the oxide layer
                                 ↓
After 1000 erases:
   ┌─────────┐
   │ Floating Gate │ Electronics
   ├─────────┤
   │ SiO₂ │ ● ● ● ← Increase in trap states and capture electrons
   │ ● ● │ ● ●
   │ ● ● ● │ ● ● ← The trap state forms a conductive path
   ├─────────┤
   │Substrate │
   └─────────┘

After 100,000 erases:
   ┌─────────┐
   │ Floating Gate │ Electronics
   ├─────────┤
   │ SiO₂ │ ← The oxide layer is severely degraded
   │ ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓ │ ← The trap states are so dense that they form a leakage path
   │ ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓ │ ← Electrons can escape through trap-assisted tunneling (TAT)
   ├─────────┤
   │Substrate │
   └─────────┘
```

The thickness of the tunnel oxide layer is about 10 nanometers, which is the thickness of dozens of atoms. Every time an electron passes through it, it leaves microscopic structural defects (dangling bonds, oxygen vacancies, interface states) on this extremely thin barrier. These defects can trap electrons, forming "traps." As the number of erasing and writing increases, more and more trap states are formed, forming a trap-assisted tunneling (TAT) channel. Electrons can no longer tunnel through the complete oxide layer barrier through pure FN tunneling, but "jump" through one trap state after another, easily leaking from the floating gate back to the substrate.

The erase and write life of SLC Flash (each cell stores 1 bit) is usually calibrated to 100,000 times. After this number of times, the trap states on the tunnel oxide layer have become dense enough to significantly increase the charge leakage rate, and the data retention time is drastically reduced from decades to months or less. **

This is not a "broken" moment, this is a **progressive process of deterioration**. Your 99999th erase and the first erase use the same layer of tunnel oxide, but this layer of oxide has been completely different after experiencing 99998 tunneling impacts. It is thinner (some oxygen atoms have been knocked out), dirtier (full of defects), and more leaky (trap-assisted tunneling channels have been formed).

Wear and tear is common: It's not just Flash.- The magnetic heads of old-fashioned mechanical hard disks must float a few nanometers above the disk when reading, without making contact. However, the occasional "bump" will physically scrape off the magnetic coating on the disk, resulting in bad sectors.
- The fibers of the paper undergo slight bending fatigue every time the page is turned. In a book that has been paged a hundred thousand times, the paper will break at the crease.
- Even the clay tablets are not perfect, and the indentations of cuneiform writing are three-dimensional, but if the clay tablets are repeatedly scratched (Sumerian students used clay tablets to practice calligraphy repeatedly, smoothed them after writing, and then wrote again), the surface will become thinner and more uneven, until it can no longer be written.

To write is to wear. This equation is the underlying curse of almost any reliable storage system. Your file system is saying "write a file" and the physical world is saying "you gave the oxide one more punch".

## Library 1: The bookshelves are aging and the beams are bowed

In the “library” metaphor, wear and tear is the aging of the bookshelf.

You manage a library. You program the first bookshelf area (program a piece of Flash), and you put books on it (write data). The next day, you decide to clear out, rebuild (erase), and rearrange your bookshelf. On the third day, you clean and edit again... By the 100,001th time you have rebuilt your bookshelf, your bookshelf is no longer strong. The surface of the partition is full of cracks (trap state), and the wood chips in the screw holes have begun to fall off (the oxide layer has become thinner). The bookshelf is no longer a bookshelf, it has become a shell full of gaps, and a slight earthquake can cause books to slip out of it.

Wear leveling, one of the core algorithms in the Flash file system, essentially rotates all the bookshelf areas in the entire library to ensure that no one shelf is removed too many times. You are the "cataloging manager" of the library. You have a general catalog of the collection (free_bitmap), which records the status of each bookshelf: which ones are brand new (erased and waiting to be written), which ones are full (already programmed), and which ones need to be recataloged (to be erased and recycled). Every time you prepare to organize a new collection of books, you will look for the bookshelf on the general catalog that has been "disassembled the least frequently", that is, the free block with the least wear. You won't toss the same bookshelf over and over again, forcing it to deconstruct and collapse after a hundred thousand reorganizations. You will spread the loss and let every bookshelf in the entire library share the life cost equally.

## Original Sin 2: Interruption - writing is not an atomic operation

The second sin is more insidious than the first sin, wear and tear is **progressive aging**, while interruption is **instantaneous disaster**.

Let’s first figure out one fact: How long does it take to erase Flash?

```
Flash erase timeline

Erase command issued
  │ 0ms
  ├─ Erase voltage establishment (internal charge pump boost) ~100μs
  ├─ Full block erase (FN tunneling pulls out electrons) ~2-5ms
  ├─ Erase verification (read page by page to confirm that all are "1") ~1-3ms
  ├─ Weak programming repair (repaired erased unit) ~1-2ms
  │ Complete
  └─Total: NAND blocks ~2-5ms; NOR 4KB sectors typically 50-200ms
```

NAND block erase takes about 2-5ms; NOR sectors typically 50-200ms. It takes about 100ms for a person to blink. In the gap between Flash performing erasure without blinking an eye, you can't intervene, you can only wait.

And what if the power is cut off within these 5 milliseconds?

The answer depends on the design of the Flash chip and its internal protection circuit, but **under general circumstances, an interrupted erase operation will leave the target block in a "partially erased" state**. Some pages have all become "1"s (erased), and some pages still have old data (no time to erase). The data is neither available nor identifiable as "erased", it is a crater with residual content that cannot be correctly interpreted.

For write (programming) operations, the situation is similar:

```
Flash programming timeline

Programming instructions are issued
  │ 0ms
  ├─ Programming voltage establishment ~50μs
  ├─ Hot carrier injection/FN tunneling writing electrons ~200-500μs
  ├─ Programming verification (read back to confirm that the threshold voltage reaches the standard) ~50μs
  │ Complete
  └─ Total: about 300-600μs (typ./per page)

⚠ If power is lost during writing:
   - The target page may be in a "half-programmed" state - the threshold voltage is between "1" and "0"
   - Not sure what this page is supposed to be about
   - When reading out, it may read "1" today and "0" tomorrow (threshold drift)
```

Note: The minimum writing unit of Flash is **page** (Page). When your writing on a page is interrupted, all cells on this page may be in an uncertain threshold voltage state. Some electrons are injected into the floating gate but have not completed stabilization, and others should be injected but have not yet started. You can't rely on these numbers because they don't have a bottom line.But the deeper problem here is: **The user write operation is logically "a file" and may be physically divided into multiple pages of programming operations. ** If you want to write a 2000-byte file in Flash, and the page size of this Flash is 256 bytes, then this "logical write" actually contains about 8 physical page programming operations. If there is a power outage between pages 5 and 6, the first 5 pages have been written (physically completed), but pages 6 to 8 are still in an indeterminate state. The integrity of the file is compromised.

This is the problem with write non-atomicity. Logically "one" write operation is physically "multiple" indivisible independent operations. If power is lost in the middle of the sequence, there is an inconsistency between the file and the metadata.

For mechanical hard disks, the problem is more intuitive: when the magnetic head is powered off during the writing process, it will stop at an intermediate position, the magnetization transition zone is cut off, and adjacent sectors are disturbed by the residual magnetic field, causing not only the target sector, but also adjacent sectors to be damaged.

## Library 2: Power outage during cataloging

In the "library" metaphor, an interruption is a sudden blackout during the cataloging process.

Imagine you are organizing a bookshelf. You have just reached the third level (5 physical pages have been written), the labels have not been attached, and the books have not been placed firmly. Sudden power outage. The bookshelf is dark.

Not only the half of the bookshelf you haven't finished editing will be darkened, but the books that have been arranged may also be distorted. Programmed adjacent pages are disturbed by residual high voltage (the hard disk's write magnetic field spills into adjacent sectors), and erased blocks are in a partially programmed fuzzy state.

Now that your file system has restarted, you need to ask yourself a few questions:
- Should this bookshelf be continued?
- Which of the books (written physical pages) on this side of the bookshelf are good and which are half-finished?
- Are the old bookshelf (old version of file data) still there?

The last question leads to the core design principle of log-structured file systems: **Copy-on-Write. Old data is never overwritten in place. ** (Note that this is a term in the field of file systems, not the copy-on-write method of fork() in virtual memory: each write is written to a new location, and the pointer points to the new data after the new data is completely written to the disk. In this way, even if the power is interrupted during the writing process, the old data is still intact). When you organize the new bookshelf, the old bookshelf is still standing next to it intact. You don't take down the old bookshelf until the new bookshelf is compiled, the labels are attached, and the inventory is passed. If a power outage comes and the new bookshelf collapses, you still have the old bookshelf to return. If the user loses power mid-write, you roll back to a previous state where the file and metadata match.

Your SB Log (SuperBlock log) plays the role of a "borrowing record book" in this process: every time you start a new operation, you first write "Start cataloging bookshelf A" in the record book; halfway through cataloging the bookshelf, you record "Progress: 50%" in the record book; when the bookshelf is finished, you write "Cataloging bookshelf A completed." After restarting, you don't need to guess the status of the bookshelf, you can just flip through the record book. If the record book says "Start Cataloging Shelf A" but not "Catalog Shelf A Complete," you know that this shelf was not finished when the power went out, so remove it and start over from the old shelf.

## Original Sin 3: Noise - environmental interference causes data to deteriorate

The first two original sins are caused by you **actively**. You write data, which causes wear and tear; the power is interrupted during your writing process, which causes inconsistency. But the third original sin is something you bear passively. If you do nothing, the data will quietly deteriorate.

**Charge leakage. **

This is the most fundamental data retention problem of Flash. The electrons in the floating gate are locked by the SiO₂ insulating layer, but this "lock" is not perfect. The electrons leak back to the substrate through the tunnel oxide layer at an extremely slow but never-stop speed. The higher the temperature, the faster the leakage, because the thermal kinetic energy of the electrons increases and there is a greater probability of crossing the barrier of the oxide layer.

```
The relationship between Flash data retention time and temperature

Temperature Data retention time (SLC, new chip)
25°C → 100+ years (theoretical calculation)
55°C → about 10-15 years
85°C → about 1-3 years
125°C → about a few months

Accelerated degradation after wear:
SLC, 100,000 erase cycles, 85°C → data retention may only be a few months
MLC (2 bits per cell), new chip, 85°C → about 1-3 years (narrower threshold window, easier to misread)
```

Temperature not only accelerates leakage, but uneven heat distribution causes cells in different physical locations to leak at different rates. Cells written in the center of the chip (with poor heat dissipation and high temperature) may leak faster than cells at the edges. The data is correct when you write it. If you read the same data back a year later, some bits may have been flipped.

**Read interference. **

This problem is even more counter-intuitive than charge leakage: reading without writing can also corrupt data. **

When you read the same Flash page repeatedly, you continuously apply a high voltage to the control gates of all unselected cells (forcing them to "turn on" to allow current to pass). Although that voltage is not high enough to trigger programming, it is enough to weakly inject electrons into the floating gate. This process is extremely slow, but if the same page is read hundreds of thousands of times, the accumulated injected electrons are enough to change the threshold voltage of some cells. Data on adjacent pages is silently overwritten by read operations.

This is not a design flaw, it is an inevitable result of the tunneling effect of quantum mechanics at the practical level. You don't want to inject electrons? Whether you think about it or not, as long as the electric field is large enough, electrons have a non-zero probability of tunneling.

**Cosmic rays and single particle flipping. **

This is the most magical one. A high-energy particle (proton, neutron, heavy ion) from outer space penetrates the atmosphere, passes through your chip package, passes through the silicon substrate, and ionizes a string of electron-hole pairs along its flight path. These free charges are collected by the electric field of the memory cell, the DRAM capacitor is charged/discharged (bit flip), the voltage of the SRAM storage node is disturbed (bit flip), and additional electrons are injected into the Flash floating gate (threshold voltage shift).

```
Types of Single Event Effects (SEE)

Single event upset (SEU): high-energy particle → capacitor charge/discharge → bit changes from 1 to 0 (or vice versa)
                     Impact: DRAM, SRAM, registers, latches

Single Event Transient (SET): Energetic particles → transient voltage spikes at combinatorial logic outputs
                     If this spike happens to be sampled by the clock edge → it is equivalent to one SEU

Single event gate penetration (SEGR): High energy particles → breakdown MOSFET gate oxide layer → permanent damage
                     Influence: Flash, power MOSFET (the enemy of aerospace-grade devices)

Single Event Functional Interrupt (SEFI): High-energy particle → hits control logic → chip enters error state
                     Need to power on and reset again to recover
```

This effect is extremely significant at aircraft cruising altitude (about 10,000 meters), where the atmosphere is thinner and the flux of cosmic rays is hundreds of times that on the ground. The Flash storage of the avionics system must be triple-modularly redundant (important data is stored in three copies, and two out of three are voted to select the correct value when reading), or a Radiation-Hardened (radiation-resistant reinforced) special process chip must be used, but Rad-Hard's Flash is hundreds of times more expensive than consumer-grade Flash.

On the ground, the flip rate is on the order of a few bits per GB per day, sounds like a small amount? A 128GB mobile phone storage may have dozens of bits flipped by cosmic rays every day. In your selfie, the color of a few pixels changes quietly every day, but you can't see it on the screen, because a photo has 12 million pixels, and a change of one or two pixels cannot be detected by the naked eye. But in critical systems, such as car brake controller parameters stored in Flash, a bit flip may lead to an error in brake pressure calculation, which is fatal.

**Temperature fluctuations. **

The threshold voltage of Flash is sensitive to temperature. When reading Flash, you need to add a reference voltage to the control gate to determine whether the threshold voltage is above or below the reference voltage, but the threshold voltage itself will drift with temperature. If you write data at 25°C and then read it at 85°C, the threshold voltage may have drifted to the other side of the reference voltage, causing the read result to be incorrect.

This is why there is usually a parameter called "cross temperature coefficient" in the data sheet of NOR Flash. The greater the difference between the writing temperature and the reading temperature, the higher the bit error rate. This is also why high-end Flash controllers perform adaptive reference voltage calibration when reading, detect threshold voltage distribution in real time, and dynamically adjust the read judgment level.

## Rowhammer: You visit your neighbor and your neighbor gets beaten up

In 2014, a study from Carnegie Mellon University and Intel revealed a shocking flaw in DRAM: **Rowhammer**.

The principle is surprisingly simple: the storage capacitance of a DRAM cell is very small (tens of femtofarads), and the physical distance between adjacent rows is very close. When you repeatedly activate ("hammer") a certain row of DRAM at an extremely high frequency, the word line voltage of this row repeatedly switches on and off, causing electromagnetic interference, causing **coupling leakage** in the storage capacitance of adjacent rows, and the data in adjacent rows is forcibly flipped because of your high-frequency access.

```
Rowhammer attack mechanism

Attack Row:
  │ ┌────Word line─────┐
  │ │ ┌───┬───┬──│─┬───┐
  ├──┼──│ ■ │ ■ │ ■││ │ ■ │ ← Each activation: word line voltage rises to Vpp
  │ │ ├───┼───┼──│┼─┼───┤ High voltage produces electromagnetic interference
  │ │ │ ■ │ ■ │ ■││ │ ■ │
  │ │ ├───┼───┼──│┼─┼───┤
Victim Row:
  │ │ ├───┼───┼──│┼─┼───┤ ← Capacitive coupling: The charge state of adjacent capacitors is affected
  ├──┼──│ Δ │ Δ │ Δ││ │ Δ │ bit flip: 1→0 or 0→1
  │ │ └───┴───┴──│┴─┴───┘
  │ └──────────────┘

Attack conditions:
- >50,000 activations of attack row within 64ms refresh window
- DDR3 is the most vulnerable, DDR4 is partially mitigated through TRR (Target Row Refresh)
- Particularly dangerous in a virtualized environment (the virtual machine can trigger the host's Rowhammer)
```

Rowhammer is shocking because it exposes a deeper problem: the collapse of the isolation assumption. ** The traditional assumption in the design of digital circuits, that the physical state of one memory cell does not affect adjacent cells, no longer holds true after extreme manufacturing process scaling. When the unit spacing shrinks to tens of nanometers, the capacitive coupling, inductive coupling, and thermal coupling between adjacent units can no longer be ignored. When you access address A, the data at address B has nothing to do with you logically and has been physically changed.

It was a brutal capitulation of the digital world to the analog world.

## SEE in Avionics: Humans “see” cosmic rays

On October 7, 2008, a Qantas A330 passenger plane flying from Singapore to Australia encountered a violent attitude loss at cruising altitude over the Indian Ocean. The plane nosed down suddenly without any input from the pilot, and passengers and flight attendants were thrown into the ceiling, injuring hundreds of people.

Later investigation found that: in the aircraft's ADIRU (Atmospheric Data Inertial Reference Unit), a bit storing data words was flipped by high-energy cosmic particles. This bit happens to be a key status flag. After flipping, the autopilot executed the wrong flight control command.

This incident caused the entire aerospace industry to re-evaluate the risk level of "single event upset" in commercial avionics equipment. Previously, it was only systematically considered in spacecraft design. The cruising altitude of a civil airliner is precisely the area hardest hit by cosmic rays.

The solutions for aviation-grade storage equipment are layered:
1. Data storage has three-module redundancy, and important parameters are stored in three copies.
2. ECC/EDC error correction code, detect and correct single bit flips
3. Periodic data scrubbing (Scrubbing), the background reads all storage locations one by one, detects and repairs flipped bits
4. Physical shielding, adding metal caps to key chips to shield high-energy particles
5. Radiation-Hardened process, using SOI (silicon-on-insulator substrate) and special well structure to reduce charge collection

But the mobile phone in your hand, a product that costs less than one ten thousandth of an aviation-grade device, does not have these. Your mobile phone's Flash only relies on the manufacturer's built-in ECC (BCH code or LDPC code) to correct errors within a few dozen bits. Once the number of error bits exceeds the error correction capability of ECC, the data will be permanently damaged.

**This exposes a cruel fact: You feel that "my photos are saved well" because countless layers of algorithms and error correction mechanisms are desperately saving your data in places you don't know. You live in a sense of security built on multiple layers of "lies", and the file system is the layer closest to the user in this lie system. **

## Key Insight: Storage is not about “storing data”, storage is about “building reliability”

So far, we have seen the three original sins of storage: wear, interruption, and noise. They are not design flaws, they are inevitable constraints that the physical world imposes on any information retention system.

This leads to a fundamental cognitive transformation:

**The essence of storage is not to "store data". The essence of storage is to build reliable data abstraction on unreliable physical media. **

Simply "write it in and read it out" is not enough. The medium wears out as the data is written, the power may be cut off as the data is written, or it may be flipped over by cosmic rays if left unmoved. You must **proactively combat these damaging factors**, combat aging through wear leveling, combat interruptions through logging and CoW, and combat noise through ECC and verification.

This is the true meaning of the existence of file systems.

Many people think that the file system was invented "to make it easier for you to use files". It is for human convenience to give data a name (file) and put it in a folder (directory).But that's not the deepest raison d'etre of file systems. The deepest rationale for a file system is this: It creates a wall between you and unreliable physical media. You see neat files and directories on this side of the wall, such as "photo.jpg is in the DCIM folder." What about the other side of the wall? On the other side of the wall is a violent, chaotic physical world where electrons are leaking, oxide layers are cracking, power is randomly cut off, and cosmic particles are passing through. This wall is the file system.

The file system is a **promise** between the user and the physical media: 'Don't worry, your data is fine'. Then it uses extremely complex engineering methods to do its best to fulfill this promise.

## Another Perspective: The "Triangular Dilemma" of Storage

If you put the three original sins together, you will see that all storage solutions are chosen in a triangle dilemma. You can never meet all three needs at the same time:

```
Storage Trilemma

              Durable (long life)
              /\
             / \
            / \
           /\
          /storage\
         /Project\
        / \
Convenient (large capacity/─────────── Reliable (anti-interruption/anti-noise)
 fast)

Clay tablet: Durable ✓✓✓ Convenient ✗ Reliable ✓ (Easy to deal with interruptions - finalize the shape after carving)
Papyrus: Durable ✓ Convenient ✓✓ Reliable ✗ (Destroys when exposed to water)
Core: Durable ✓✓✓ Convenient ✗✗ Reliable ✓✓✓
DRAM: Durable ✗ Convenient ✓✓✓ Reliable ✗ (Volatile)
NOR Flash: Durable ✓✓ Convenient ✓ Reliable ✓✓
NAND Flash: Durable ✓ Convenient ✓✓✓ Reliable ✓ (FTL+ECC required)
```

Each medium occupies a place in this triangle. No one can get full marks at the same time. If you pursue extreme durability (thousand-year archives), you have to accept bulky and low-capacity clay tablets and miniatures. If you pursue extreme speed and capacity (mobile phone storage), you have to accept NAND Flash with limited life and the need for error-correcting code-assisted reliability.

This is the bottom line laid by the physical world for all storage engineers. You will always make trade-offs in the triangle dilemma. You can never win. You can only choose a way to lose that is acceptable to you. **

## Library Three: Bookworms and Mold—Metaphors of Noise

Going back to the metaphor of "library", noise is the moths and mold that live in your library.

The data is written, the bookshelf is organized, and the labels are attached. You turn off the lights, go to get off work with peace of mind, and feel that everything is fine. But what you don’t know is that something is moving in the night.

**Bookworm (charge leakage)** Take a sneaky nibble on the pages of your book every day. A small bite a day, a book a year. If you don’t check regularly, you can’t see it. But one day you opened the book collection and it completely shattered. This "bookworm's" method is so gentle that you can sleep peacefully without checking for a year. The data retention problem of Flash is like this. Bit by bit is quietly turned over, and it is only discovered after the accumulation exceeds the threshold of the ECC error correction capability.

**Mold (read interference and cosmic rays)** They are eating away at your call labels. A little blurry today, a little blurry tomorrow. As you walk through that hallway over and over (reading the same page over and over again), the mold smells moisture and becomes more active. When a high-energy particle hits, it's like a piece of cement falling from the ceiling. The label of the location where it hits is completely blurred. No warning, no predictability, you can only fix it afterwards (error correction code correction, restore from backup).

**Wear leveling, ECC, and power-off protection** are your security measures: Wear leveling allows you to no longer reuse the same old bookshelf, ECC helps you repair call labels blurred by mold, and power-off protection ensures that the library can return to its previous intact state after a power outage.
