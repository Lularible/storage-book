# 2.1 Floating-Gate Transistors: A Lamp That Never Goes Out

## You have a bucket in your hand, but it doesn’t leak

Imagine you have a sealed bucket in your hand. You pour the water in, put the lid on, and throw it into storage. You come back a year later, open the lid, and the water is still there.

This bucket defies your everyday physical intuition. Your water glass will evaporate if left open, your water pipe joints will leak, and your plastic bottles will taste bad if left for too long. But this bucket doesn’t leak a drop.

Because it doesn't need to be "plugged" to prevent leaks. It uses a physical law, namely the quantum mechanical tunneling effect, to create a potential well that "needs conditions to enter and conditions to exit."

**This is a floating gate transistor. **

Before the birth of Flash, all non-volatile storage came at a price: EPROM (Ultraviolet Erasable Programmable Read-Only Memory) requires 20 minutes of UV light to erase, and you have to point the quartz window on the chip at the UV lamp; EEPROM (Electrically Erasable Programmable Read-Only Memory) can be electrically erased, but the typical unit is 2T (a floating gate storage tube plus a selection tube), which has a large area, small capacity, and high cost.

In 1984, Toshiba's Fujio Shioka invented Flash memory. His core innovation is: **Utilizing the Fowler-Nordheim tunneling effect to allow electrons to "traverse" the insulating layer under high pressure. No ultraviolet light is required, and there is no need for two additional transistors per unit. The structure is simple and the density is extremely high. **

The Chinese translation of Flash is "flash memory". The word "flash" comes from a description by his colleague Shoji Ariizumi: the erasure process was "as fast as a camera flash." Prior to this, EPROM erasure took 20 minutes, while Flash erasure only took 1 second.

From that moment on, non-volatile storage entered the "flash" era.

## The forgotten predecessor: EEPROM

On the eve of the birth of Flash, EEPROM (Electrically Erasable Programmable Read-Only Memory) dominated the embedded storage world for more than 20 years. The basic principle of EEPROM is almost the same as that of Flash, that is, floating gate transistor plus F-N tunneling, but there is a key difference: **EEPROM can be erased on a byte basis. **

This means that you can directly modify any byte in the EEPROM without erasing the entire sector. Sound perfect?

The problem is structural. Each cell of EEPROM requires two transistors: a storage transistor to store charge, and a select transistor for byte-level addressing. Each unit of Flash only requires one transistor. The subtlety of Fujio Fujioka is that he deleted the selection transistor.

```
Structural differences between EEPROM vs Flash
=======================

EEPROM unit:
  Each cell has two transistors:
    ┌───┐ ┌───┐
    │Storage│ │Select│ ← Select transistor for byte-level addressing
    │Cell│ │Tube │ Enables each byte to be erased independently
    └───┘ └───┘
  
  Cost: Area × 2 (two transistors vs one)
       The cost per bit is much higher than Flash

Flash unit:
   ┌───┐
   │Storage│ ← Single transistor unit, smallest area
   │Unit│ But erasure must be in sector units
   └───┘

Although the selection transistor solves the problem of "byte-level erasure",
But EEPROM will never be able to beat Flash in terms of cost.
This is the most common trade-off in engineering: flexibility vs. cost.
```

EEPROM still exists today and is used in data storage scenarios that require small and frequent modifications, such as mileage, configuration parameters, and fault codes of vehicle ECUs. Its typical capacity is a few KB to tens of KB. Flash has gained more than a hundred times the capacity advantage at the cost of "giving up byte-level erasure".

Your ECU may have both an EEPROM (to store operating parameters) and a NOR Flash (to store firmware and large blocks of data). EEPROM handles small data that needs to be modified frequently, and Flash handles firmware and logs. Each performs its own duties and bears the consequences of its own physical choices.

## Turn on a floating gate transistor

The structure of a floating gate transistor is essentially a MOSFET with two gates:

```
Floating gate transistor physical structure and circuit symbols
=====================================

  Structural cross-section diagram: Circuit symbol (simplified):

     Control grid CG ──────────┐
  ┌─────────────────────┐ ┌─── Drain
  │═══ ONO insulation layer ══════││ ← Blocks electrons from escaping │
  ├──────────────────────┤ │ ┌──── CG
  │ ★Floating Gate FG ★ ││ ← Electronic Prison ├──┤
  ├──────────────────────┤ │ └──── FG (implicit)
  │══ Tunnel Oxide (~8nm)══││ ← Quantum Tunnel │
  ├────────┬─────────────┤ └─── Source
  │ N+ source │ N+ drain │
  │ (S) │ (D) │
  └────────┴─────────────┘
      P-type substrate (channel area)
```

Five-layer structure, from bottom to top:1. **P-type silicon substrate**, this is the foundation. Boron-doped silicon conducts electricity through holes. The source and drain are N+ heavily doped regions, like two islands embedded in a P-type ocean.
2. **Tunnel oxide layer**, about 8nm thick silicon dioxide. Thin enough, electrons can tunnel through energy quanta under high pressure; thick enough, electrons do not have enough energy to cross under normal pressure. This is a "one-way channel" for the entire device.
3. **Floating Gate**, polysilicon conduction band. It is completely wrapped by ONO and tunnel oxide layers on top, bottom, left, right, front and back, without any electrical connections. This is an absolute "isolated island". Once the electrons are inside, they are trapped inside.
4. **ONO stack (Oxide-Nitride-Oxide)**, about 15nm thick SiO₂/Si₃N₄/SiO₂ three-layer insulation structure. The leakage current of ONO is three to four orders of magnitude smaller than that of pure SiO₂. It ensures that the electrons in the floating gate will not escape from it for decades.
5. **Control Gate**, polysilicon electrode, external word line. It "indirectly" controls the floating gate potential through capacitive coupling, because physically, CG and FG are separated with a 15nm ONO in between.

Here's the key: **CG and FG are physically isolated, but electrically coupled. **

This means that you can change the potential of FG "through ONO" by controlling the voltage on CG, thereby controlling the conduction state of the channel. The charge on the FG can be maintained for decades without external voltage since there is no discharge channel.

## Write: Quantum tunneling - electrons are "passing through the wall"

Writing is called **Program** in Flash terminology. Its purpose is to send electrons into the floating gate.

But the floating gate is surrounded by insulator and oxide in all directions. According to classical physics, it is impossible for electrons to cross the oxide barrier (SiO₂ forbidden band is about 9eV, but the electron tunneling barrier is about 3.1eV; about 120 times compared with 0.026eV).

Quantum mechanics gives a way: **Fowler-Nordheim Tunneling (F-N Tunneling)**.

In classical physics, when an electron with energy E encounters a potential barrier with height V₀ (V₀ > E), it will definitely bounce back. Just like if you throw a tennis ball at the wall, the wall is stronger than the force you throw, and the ball will definitely bounce back.

But in quantum mechanics, the electron is a **wave function**. The wave function decreases exponentially in the potential barrier and will not decay to zero instantly. If the barrier is thin enough, the wave function "leaks" out from the other side of the barrier, and the electron crosses it.

```
Schematic diagram of F-N tunneling principle
=================

  (a) No bias voltage: (b) Add positive high voltage to CG:
  
  energy ↑ energy ↑
       │ ┌─────────────┐ │ ┌─────────────┐
       │ │ Potential Barrier (V₀) │ │ │ Potential Barrier (Thinning!)│
       │ │ │ │ │ ┌──────┐ │
  ─────┤ │ │ ─────┤ /│ │Tunnel! │ │
  Eₑ │→ │ │ Eₑ │→ / │ │ → │ │
  ─────┤ │ │ ─────┤ / │ └──────┘ │
       └────┤ Tunnel oxide layer │ └────┤ │
            │ (SiO₂ 8nm) │ │ │
            └─────────────┘ └──────────────┘
  
  Electron energy < barrier height → the electrons are bounced back and the high voltage causes the barrier to "tilt and thin" → the electrons tunnel through!
```

Take FN/ISPP programming of NAND as an example: apply high voltage to the control gate, ground the channel, and allow electrons to tunnel into the floating gate. NOR's CHE programming voltage/bias is different. This high voltage increases the potential of the floating gate through capacitive coupling, forming an extremely strong electric field (about 10MV/cm level!) between the floating gate and the channel.

Under this electric field, the energy band of the tunnel oxide layer tilts, and the potential barrier changes from a rectangle to a triangle. The base width of the triangle is much thinner than the original 8nm. Electrons in the channel have a high enough probability to tunnel through this thinned barrier and enter the floating gate.

Once the high pressure is removed, the barrier returns to its original shape and height. The electrons in the floating gate are trapped, and they don't have enough energy to tunnel out again.

**Electronic went in and the door was closed. **

## Erase: drive electrons out

Erasing is to drive out the electrons in the floating gate. F-N tunneling is also used, but in the opposite direction.

The erasing method of NOR Flash is to apply high voltage to the source and connect the control gate to ground (or negative voltage):

```
Erase process
=========

         CG = 0V (or negative voltage)
    ┌──────────────────────┐
    │═══ ONO ═════════════││
    ├──────────────────────┤
    │ ★▉▉▉▉▉▉★ ││ ← Electrons in the floating gate
    ├──────────────────────┤
    │══ Tunnel Oxide ═══════││
    ├────────┬─────────────┤
    │ S=+12V │ D=Floating/0V │
    │ ↑High pressure │ │
    │Electrons tunnel from the floating gate back to the source→ │
    └────────┴─────────────┘
    
    Electron path: floating gate → tunnel oxide → source
```

Apply high voltage (about +12V) to the source and connect the control gate to ground. The strong electric field between the floating gate and the source causes the energy band of the tunnel oxide layer to tilt again, and this time electrons tunnel from the floating gate side back to the source.Note: NOR Flash erasure is performed by **sector**, usually a group of 4KB to 64KB, and is erased at one time. You can't just erase a byte. This is because the erase voltage needs to be applied to the source lines of all cells in the entire sector, and the source lines are common.

This is one of the characteristics of NOR Flash: **fast writing, slow erasing** (erasing a 4KB sector may take 100ms).

## Read: Guess the weight of the bucket

No electrons can be read in or out. It just "weighs the floating gate" to see if there are electrons in it.

The specific method is: add a read voltage V_read between the erase state threshold voltage and the programming state threshold voltage to the control gate, and add a small voltage (~1V) to the drain.

```
Reading principle
=========

           ┌─ V_read ───┐
           │CG │
  ┌────────┼──────────────┼──┐
  │ ONO │
  │ ★FG★ │ ← FG charge = ?
  │ Tunnel oxide layer │
  │ ┌─────┬─────┐ │
  │ │ S │ D │ Vd=1V │
  │ │ │ │←───────┤
  │ │ │ Read! │ │
  │ └─────┴─────┘ │
  │P-sub │
  └────────────────────────┘
  
  Erase state (FG has no electrons): Vth low → V_read > Vth → channel conduction → read "1"
  Programming state (FG has electrons): Vth high → V_read < Vth → channel cutoff → read "0"
  
  Note: In NOR Flash logic, "Erase state=1, Programming state=0"
```

When there are no electrons in the floating gate, the threshold voltage Vth is very low (for example, 3V). You set V_read to 5V, 5V > 3V, the channel is turned on, and current flows out of the drain. Read "1".

When there are electrons in the floating gate, the electric field generated by those electrons will offset part of the electric field of the control gate, and the threshold voltage Vth becomes higher (for example, 7V). Your V_read is still 5V, 5V < 7V, the channel is not conducting, and there is no current in the drain. Read "0".

**Reads usually do not change the floating gate charge, but are not infinite: there are read disturbances and a limit on the number of reads. NOR's read endurance is much higher than write/erase, but it is still subject to the data sheet. **

## Bucket Metaphor: Why “Not Leaking” Is a Miracle

Think of the floating gate as a sealed bucket:

- **Programming** = Fill a bucket with water (inject electrons). You use F-N tunneling to "press" electrons from the channel into the floating gate with high voltage, and there is water in the bucket.
- **Erase** = Turn the bucket upside down and expel the electrons from the same entrance. You can't just pour a drop, the whole bucket has to be emptied.
- **Read** = Weigh the bucket. There are electrons in the bucket → heavy → high threshold voltage → read “0”. There are no electrons in the bucket → light → low threshold voltage → read “1”. Weighing usually does not change the water volume, but long-term/repeated readings will cause read interference; the reading life is not unlimited.
- **Wear** = Thinning of barrel walls. Every time water is poured and poured, electrons will destroy some Si-O bonds as they travel through the tunnel oxide layer, forming trap states, tiny leak points. The more trap states there are, the greater the leakage. Eventually the barrel wall becomes perforated and the barrel becomes useless.

**This bucket is already leaking. It just leaks very slowly, so slowly that you think it doesn't leak. **

You may ask: Since a transistor can only distinguish between two states: "with water" and "without water", can it accurately control the amount of water and store more bits in one unit? sure. But for every additional bit, the sensing margin is compressed by one level. This problem runs through all the physical dilemmas from SLC to QLC, and will be discussed in a special section later.

Now, let's put aside the question of "how many bits can be stored in a unit" and ask a more basic question: How are billions of such units connected on a silicon chip?

## A light that never goes out

You close the casing of the Flash chip in your hand. The silicon chip is encapsulated inside with black glue so you can't see anything. But you know, there are billions of floating-gate transistors in there, and each one of them is a little light.

These lights require no power source to operate. The 0s and 1s you write in indicate whether there are electrons in the floating gate. Electrons are in that 8nm oxide prison, and they can stay there for decades if no one opens the door to the high voltage.

This does not violate the second law of thermodynamics. This is a metastable state, just like a stone on the top of a mountain that doesn't roll down immediately, but it doesn't roll down forever. Rolling down requires a little initial disturbance. For floating gates, that disturbance is the trap state in the oxide layer. When there are enough trap states, the "slope" of the stone becomes large enough and electrons escape.

But you don't care about that. You only know that the moment you write a line of code, a text message, or a password, billions of electrons are driven into billions of small quantum prisons. They just stay there quietly, day and night, waiting to be read one day in the future.**You either read or erase. Otherwise, they just light up silently in time. **
