# 3.1 Thought Experiment: Managing 100 Sheets of Paper

## You have a hundred pieces of paper in your hand, and you can only write on each piece once.

Imagine this: you are an office clerk. You have a blank notebook. No, to be precise, you have a hundred loose sheets of A4 paper. You can write on any piece of paper. You have a pen. Your job is simple: record each visitor's name, time, and reason, one line per visit, then file the sheet.

But the problem is, your boss likes to change his mind. At 10 o'clock in the morning, he registered "Zhang San, visiting the purchasing department." At 11 o'clock, he sent someone to say, "No, he is visiting the finance department." You have to change.

In a regular notebook, you take an eraser and erase "Purchasing Department" and write "Finance Department." Completed in one second.

But on your desk, each piece of paper can only be written once. The ink on the paper is specially prepared, and after it dries, it is permanently etched into the paper. You cannot change it in its original position. You can't use an eraser.

How do you change it? The only way is: **Get a blank piece of paper, copy all the content that you want to keep on the old paper, and write the modifications in the corresponding positions. The ink is still there on the old paper, but you don't use it anymore.**

Your desk is gradually filled with scraps of paper filled with old content.

This is the physical reality that Flash file systems face.

---

## Physical constraints of one hundred sheets of paper

The physical rules of this "Paper Flash" can be strictly defined as:

```
Flash physical constraints on paper
======================

Rule 1: Write-Once
  On a new piece of paper, you can write as many words as you like until it is full.
  But once the ink dries, you can’t add words to it—the ink will smear.

Rule 2: Clear the entire card to reuse it (Block-Erase)
  To turn a full piece of paper back into blank space,
  You have to soak the whole paper with special potion -
  The potion will dissolve all the ink stains at once.
  The paper returns to blank and can be reused.

Rule 3: Don’t clear just one corner (minimum erasing unit)
  You can't just erase the bottom right line with potion.
  The entire paper must be dipped together.
  Even if you only want to change three words.

Rule 4: One hundred pieces of paper is the upper limit
  You only have a hundred pieces of paper.
  Once it's gone, it's gone.
  You have to pretend to have unlimited freedom in a limited space.

Rule 5: Wear the paper every time you empty it (P/E Cycle)
  Every time it is soaked in potion, the paper fibers will be damaged and become thinner.
  Dip a little thinly at a time. After 10,000 dips, the paper begins to transmit light.
  Dip it a hundred thousand times - the paper will be perforated and completely useless.
```

These five rules are all the physical boundary conditions for NOR Flash file system design.

100 sheets of paper = 100 physical blocks. Each sheet = one 4KB NOR Flash sector. Empty the entire sheet = erase operation. Single write = Flash programming can only be 1→0. Modify = Copy-on-Write.

Let's play this game. See how long you can survive.

---

## The first level of the game: Innocent You

What's the easiest way?

You number each piece of paper: 1 to 100. The first row of data is written on paper No. 1. Write the second line on paper No. 2. And so on.

```
Naive solution: sequential writes
=======================

Paper No. 1: [Zhang San, 10:00, Purchasing Department]
Paper No. 2: [Li Si, 10:15, Technology Department]
Paper No. 3: [Wang Wu, 10:30, Human Resources Department]
...
```

"Zhang San wants to change it to the Finance Department? No problem." You think: Just change the "Purchasing Department" on the No. 1 paper to the "Finance Department".

But you can't, the ink has eroded into the paper. You cannot change it in its original position.

What to do? You copy the other contents to be retained on paper No. 1 to a blank paper, such as paper No. 45, and then copy the item from Zhang San, and change "Purchasing Department" to "Finance Department."

```
Paper No. 45: [Zhang San, 10:00, Finance Department] ← New record
Paper No. 1: [Zhang San, 10:00, Purchasing Department] ← Old records, discarded
```

Now you have a question: Who is going to tell you which record on the paper is "real"? You turn to paper No. 45 and see that Zhang San’s record is “Finance Department”. But if you turn to paper No. 1 another day, Zhang San writes "Purchasing Department" on it. Which one is valid?

You need to mark the paper with a "version number", or "serial number".

```
Plan Revision 2: Add Serial Number
=======================

Paper No. 45: [SEQ=002] [Zhang San, 10:00, Finance Department] ← Valid
Paper No. 1: [SEQ=001] [Zhang San, 10:00, Purchasing Department] ← Invalid (old version)

Rule: For the same record, choose the one with the larger SEQ number as the valid version.
```

You add the serial number to the paper. This is good. But here comes a new problem: when you want to find Zhang San, you have to flip from the 1st piece of paper to the 100th piece of paper, because Zhang San's record may appear on any free piece of paper.

You need a "table of contents": a special piece of paper to record which paper everyone is on.

```
Plan revision three: Add catalog paper
=======================

Catalog paper (No. 0 paper):
  [Zhang San → Paper No. 45, SEQ=002]
  [John Doe → Paper No. 2, SEQ=001]
  [Wang Wu → Paper No. 3, SEQ=001]

Paper No. 45: [Zhang San, 10:00, Finance Department]
Paper No. 2: [Li Si, 10:15, Technology Department]
Paper No. 3: [Wang Wu, 10:30, Human Resources Department]
```

Now you can quickly find each person's records. But the problem is that the catalog paper itself also needs to be modified.  Every time Zhang San's record is changed to another piece of paper, you have to update the mapping on the directory paper.

The catalog paper is also a piece of paper. It can also be written only once. Every time you change the catalog, you must write the new catalog on a new blank piece of paper, and the old catalog paper becomes waste paper.

You desperately find that you are using one thing written once to manage another thing written once. Soon, the catalog becomes the fastest paper-consuming thing in your system, because it needs to be updated every time the data is modified, and every update takes up a new piece of paper.

**This is the problem with the FAT table.**

---

## The second level of the game: learn from the smarter you

You start to reflect: Since each piece of paper can only be written once, can I write multiple records on one piece of paper, that is, multiple records on one piece of paper, appended in chronological order?

```
Append-Only
=======================

Paper No. 45:
  [Record 1: Zhang San, 10:00, Purchasing Department] ← First time writing
  [Record 2: Zhang San, 10:05, Finance Department] ← Second write-up (corrected!)
  [Record 3: John Doe, 10:15, Technical Department] ← New
  [Blank area → ← You can continue writing
```

The beauty of this solution is: **You don't need to get a new piece of paper to modify Zhang San.** You only need to add a new record "Zhang San → Finance Department" at the end of Paper No. 45. When reading, read forward from the last record on paper No. 45. The first record of "Zhang San" is the latest.

But the problem is, paper No. 45 will always be filled up one day. What should I do if it’s full?

```
Paper No. 45 (full):
  [███ onto
  [███ onto
  [███ onto
  [███ onto
  It is full and there is no space to write new additional records.
```

You need **Garbage Collection**:

```
Garbage collection process
======================

1. Pick a piece of "paper with the most garbage" - such as No. 45 paper
   Paper No. 45 contains:
     - Zhang San's 2 records (the first one is outdated and is "junk")
     - 1 record of John Doe (valid)
     - There are 3 records in total, 1 of which is garbage and 2 of which are valid.

2. Get a new piece of blank paper - such as No. 89 paper

3. Copy the two valid records on paper No. 45 to paper No. 89
   Paper No. 89:
     [Zhang San, 10:05, Finance Department] ← Only keep the latest version
     [Li Si, 10:15, Technical Department]

4. Empty No. 45 paper with potion (soak the entire paper with potion → dissolve all the ink marks)
   Paper No. 45 returns to blank and re-enters the available paper pool.

5. Paper No. 89 now has a lot of blank space and you can continue to add new records.
```

You have completed the design of the garbage collection algorithm. Congratulations.

But the problem is not over yet. Garbage recycling itself also has costs: to recycle a piece of paper (No. 45), you empty a piece of paper, write on a new piece of paper (No. 89), and write the two valid records again,**which intensifies the consumption of paper.**

Each valid record may be "moved" many times during its life cycle, from paper A to paper B, and then from paper B to paper C... Each move is an additional write. The ratio between this additional write volume and the original write volume is Write Amplification.

## The third level of the game: 100-sheet book of life and death

Stop and do a complete accounting.

Let's say you receive 100 guests every day. The original amount of information per record is 50 bytes. The capacity of each sheet is 4000 bytes. Theoretically, the total capacity of your 100 sheets of paper is 400,000 bytes, enough for 80 days.

But this is a **theoretical value**. In reality:

```
Calculation of the real life span of 100 sheets of paper
=======================

Raw writing: 100 items/day × 50 bytes = 5,000 bytes/day

But there is also:
  ✗ Modification operation: About 20% of records will be modified
    Each modification = rewrite 50 bytes (append mode) or rewrite on new paper (CoW mode)
    Write amplification in append mode ≈ 1.2

  ✗ Garbage collection: trigger GC when the paper is full
    Each GC = moving valid records + emptying the trash paper (soaked in potion → becomes blank)
    Trigger GC when the average paper utilization is 50%:
      2000 bytes released per collection = 50% (garbage)
      Write 2000 bytes at the same time = 50% (effective data migration)
      GC write amplification ≈ 1.0

  ✗ Catalog management: modification of the catalog paper itself
    Each new record = directory mapping updated
    Each directory mapping ≈ 10 bytes
    100 items per day = 1000 bytes of directory modifications

  Comprehensive daily write volume ≈ 5,000 + 1,000 + 2,000 + 1,000 = 9,000 bytes/day
  Write amplification ≈ 9,000 ÷ 5,000 = 1.8

  Actual daily write volume ≈ 9,000 bytes/day
  Theoretical daily write volume = 5,000 bytes/day
  Write amplification ≈ 1.8 times

  Total paper life: 400,000 ÷ 9,000 ≈ 44 days
  Theoretical life span 80 days
```

**Write amplification will eat up more than half of your paper life.**

That's not even counting wear and tear. 100 sheets of paper, the one you use most is the one where the catalog is stored, it is updated frequently. After you empty it 1,000 times, the paper fibers are as thin as onion skin. The paper next to it also had uneven thickness due to the number of times it was emptied, and the entire stack of paper began to deform.

```
Diagram of uneven wear
======================

No wear leveling:
  Catalog paper (No. 0): █████████████████████ Cleared 1000 times ← Almost punctured
  Paper No. 1: ██ Cleared 120 times
  Paper No. 2: ██ Cleared 150 times
  ...
  Paper No. 99: █ Cleared 1 time ← Almost new
  Problem: The directory paper is first perforated and becomes corrupted, causing the entire system to collapse.

With wear leveling:
  The number of emptying times for each paper is evenly distributed between 250 and 280 times
  All paper ages simultaneously to maximize the overall life of the system.
```

At this point, you have personally touched the three core issues of Flash file system design:

1. **Space Management** (Allocation): How to find a blank piece of paper?
2. **Garbage Collection** (Garbage Collection): How to efficiently turn paper full of waste records back into blank paper?
3. **Wear Leveling** (Wear Leveling): How to make all paper age equally quickly?

---

## From paper to Flash: mapping relationship

Every element of this thought experiment has an exact counterpart in the Flash file system:

```
Paper → Flash mapping table
======================

100 sheets of paper = 100 physical blocks (4KB each)
Each piece of paper can only be written once = programming can only be 1→0
Clear the entire paper (soaked in potion) = erase (Block Erase)
Wear paper every time you empty it = P/E Cycle consumption
Write the sequence number on paper = monotonically increasing sequence number
Catalog paper = Superblock/File Table
Append mode = Log-structured metadata
Garbage Collection = GC (Garbage Collection)
Blank paper utilization = writing amplification factor
Uneven paper emptying = uneven wear
Table of contents being emptied frequently = Hot Data problem
Paper that is almost never emptied = Cold Data problem
```

Now you can replace the word "paper" with "block", replace "clear" with "erase", and replace "copy" with "program", and what you get is a simplified version of the Flash file system design document.

## Formal definition of three core operations

The three core operations in this thought experiment can be formalized as follows:

**Operation 1: Allocate**

```
allocate(n) → returns the number of n blank sheets of paper

Algorithm (simple version):
  1. Traverse paper No. 0 to No. 99
  2. Find the first piece of paper with a status of "Blank"
  3. Mark as "used"
  4. Return number

Algorithm (wear leveling version):
  1. Traverse paper No. 0 to No. 99
  2. Find all papers with "Blank" status
  3. Select the one with the "least number of clears"
  4. Mark as "used"
  5. Return number
  6. The number of times this paper has been cleared +1
```

**Operation 2: Write/Append (Write/Append)**

```
write(paper_id, offset, data, length):
  1. Check if paper [paper_id] has enough blank space
  2. Start writing data at the [offset] position of the paper
  3. Update the offset pointer within the paper

append(paper_id, data, length):
  1. Check the current writing offset of paper [paper_id] + length ≤ paper capacity
  2. Write data at the current offset position
  3. Current offset += length
```

**Operation 3: Garbage Collect**

```
gc_compact(victim_paper_id):
  1. Choose a piece of paper with the most garbage (victim)
  2. Copy all "valid" records on this paper to a new blank paper
  3. Use potion to clear the victim paper (soak the entire paper → dissolve the ink → become blank)
  4. Change the victim paper status to "blank" and re-enter the available paper pool.
  5. Update the mapping relationship on the catalog paper
```

These three operations form the skeleton of any Flash file system. Whether you are looking at LittleFS, SPIFFS, YAFFS or KnotFS, the difference is just in the specific implementation strategies of these three operations.

## You have actually designed a file system

Let’s review what design decisions you made unknowingly while playing the “100 pieces of paper” thought experiment:

```
Your Design Decision Checklist
=======================

1. You selected the "serial number scheme" to distinguish between old and new versions.
   → The corresponding block sequence number increases monotonically

2. You selected "Table of Contents" to quickly find data location
   → Corresponds to the file table (nodes[8]) embedded in SB, and the double copies are guaranteed by the double slots of SB

3. You selected "Append Mode" to avoid clearing paper frequently
   → Log append corresponding to SB Log

4. You selected "Garbage Recycling" to turn the paper filled with waste records back into blank paper.
   → Corresponds to garbage collection (GC)

5. You start thinking about "wear leveling" to age all papers evenly
   → Corresponds to wear count and free block selection

6. You realize that the "catalog paper" itself is also hot data and requires special processing.
   → Dual copies of corresponding metadata + log
```

You haven't started writing a line of C code yet, but you have completed the core architecture design of the Flash file system. This is the power of thought experiments: **Abstract complex technical problems into a physical model that is simple enough but accurate enough to allow you to traverse the entire design space using the human brain.**

## From desk to ECU

You looked up at your desk. A hundred pieces of paper were piled randomly in front of me. Some of the paper is rough and yellowed (it has been soaked in potion too many times), some of the paper has clear handwriting (newly written), and some is stained with ink (full of additional records). Your hands are covered in potion from soaking the paper so frequently.

You’re not managing a hundred pieces of paper. What you have to manage is the 512KB NOR Flash in the automotive-grade ECU, which is 128 4KB blocks. It requires a field failure rate of 0ppm and reliable operation for 15 years in the temperature range of -40°C to +125°C.

But the core issue remains unchanged. One hundred pieces of paper is still 128 blocks. Empty paper or erase block. Potion corrosion or P/E wear. Version number or serial number. Table of contents paper is still superblock.

You stood up from your desk and walked to the test bench in the workshop. The oscilloscope's probe is clamped to the CS pin of the Flash chip. You see the SPI command sequence for each erase operation: `06, D8, 00 00 XX XX`. You see the level transition of every bit on the FPGA logic analyzer. You know, behind this is the quantum tunneling of thousands of electrons in the floating gate tunnel oxide layer.

**And you just wrote one more name on a piece of paper.**

This is the charm of the Flash file system: it translates the world of quantum physics into a "paper and potion" metaphor that humans can understand. The purpose of this book is to take you through the entire translation process, from the ink on paper to the electrons in silicon.
