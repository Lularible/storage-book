# 1.2 From Clay Tablets to Paper: Ten Thousand Years of Storage Media

## There is a piece of wet mud in your hand

Imagine a scene: In Mesopotamia in 3400 BC, on the alluvial plain between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, a Sumerian temple scribe was squatting on the steps of a step pyramid. There was a palm-sized piece of wet clay in front of him, and he held a sharpened reed in his right hand.

The temple received thirty sheep as tribute today. He wants to keep accounts.

You may ask: How does he "write"?

He used the triangular tip of the reed to press wedge-shaped indentations into the wet mud. The first mark represents "thirty", the second mark represents "sheep", and the third mark represents "tribute". After writing, he left the clay tablet in the sun for a day or put it in a kiln to bake at low temperature.

This clay tablet is his "document". Thirty sheep existed in a piece of mud as big as a palm.

When the clay dries, it becomes as hard as stone. A flood occurred, the wood rotted, the knots rotted, and people died, but the clay tablet remained. Four thousand years later, archaeologists dug it out of the sand in Iraq, swept away the dust with a brush, and took a look with a magnifying glass:

"Thirty sheep."

**The information left the scribe, traveled through four thousand years, passed through the rise and fall of dozens of civilizations, and landed in front of you. What kind of durability does this require?**

Clay tablets are not a perfect storage medium, but they solve a problem that knots cannot solve: the expression of complex semantics. Knots can only record numerical values, such as "three hundred and twenty-one sheep", but clay tablets can record "Gilgamesh said: Gaze at the wall and examine its base, are the burned bricks not eternal?". This is the first leap from "counting" to "narrative" in storage.

Let’s take a look now at how clay tablets are “stored”.

---

## Cuneiform coding system

Cuneiform's name comes from the Latin *cuneus*, meaning "wedge." The scribe pressed a sharpened reed rod into the wet mud to create a wedge-shaped indentation. Different combinations of indentations represent different syllables or words.

```
Cuneiform coding hierarchy
===================

Physical medium: Nile/Mesopotamia clay ← Free, widely available, highly malleable
Writing Instrument: Reed pen (tip sharpened into a triangle) ← Natural tool, no ink required
Coding method: Depth of indentation × angle × combination ← About 600-1000 symbols
Durability means: Sun drying or kiln firing ← Dehydration and curing, chemically irreversible
Reading method: Identify the indentation pattern with the naked eye ← No need for any decoding equipment
```

This is a complete physical coding system and is by no means a simple "writing" process. Each wedge-shaped indentation is **three-dimensional**, and its depth, width, and angle determine which syllable it represents. A skilled scribe can make dozens of wedge marks in a minute.

The most shocking thing is: the information on the clay tablet hardly requires any special "hardware" to read. Human beings four thousand years later will only need a pair of eyes to see the traces left by the scribe's finger movements one afternoon. You don't need a "driver", you don't need to worry about endianness, and you don't need to worry about incompatible file format versions.

**The backward compatibility of clay tablet storage spans the entire history of human civilization.**

But the clay tablets had a fatal flaw.

---

## The “capacity wall” of clay tablets

A standard clay tablet is usually only about the size of a palm, about 10cm × 10cm. In this area, the scribe could write about 50 to 200 cuneiform symbols, which by today's standards would be about one or two hundred bytes.

One or two hundred bytes.

Now you take a photo with your mobile phone, which is about 5MB. To put it into perspective, you would need **25,000 clay tablets** to save a selfie. The 25,000 clay tablets stacked together weighed several tons, and a camel caravan was needed to move them.

The Sumerians were certainly aware of this problem. Their solution was extremely simple but effective: **build a library**.

In the 7th century BC, the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal built the first systematic library in human history in Nineveh. Archaeologists have unearthed more than 30,000 clay tablets there, covering law, epic poetry, medicine, astronomy, astrology, and mathematics. Among them is the famous "Epic of Gilgamesh", written on twelve clay tablets, which is one of the earliest literary works known to mankind.

```
"Storage Architecture" of Ashurbanipal's Library
============================

Physical storage: Clay tablets → stored in clay pots sorted by size
Indexing system: Labeled clay tablets on the outside of clay pots → record the contents of the pots (catalogue)
Classification system: Rooms are divided according to themes (Law Hall, Epic Hall, Astronomy Hall...)
Search method: The clerk locates the specific jars and clay tablets according to the label
Redundant backup: multiple copies of important documents are stored in different locations
```

Did you see it? This is a complete storage system, which has media (mud tablets), indexes (tags), directory structure (pots and rooms), and even redundant backups (multiple transcripts). Assyrian scribes three thousand years ago were already solving the same problems that file system designers face today. The fate of this library also indicates an eternal risk of the storage system:

In 612 BC, Nineveh was captured and the library burned in a fire. But for clay tablets, fire is not the enemy. Instead, the fire burns the clay tablets into ceramics, making them stronger than the original sun-dried clay tablets. Ironically, the fire the attackers set ablaze in the city actually gave Ashurbanipal's "data" greater durability. What archaeologists excavated two thousand years later were exactly these clay tablets that had been "secondary processed" by fire.

The fate of storage media is sometimes so paradoxical.

## Gift of the Nile: Papyrus

Now let us leave the Mesopotamia and go south along the Mediterranean to reach the birthplace of another ancient civilization, the Nile Valley.

The ancient Egyptians faced a completely different physical environment. There is inexhaustible clay in the Mesopotamia, but not in Egypt. Egypt is full of papyrus, an aquatic plant that can grow to four or five meters high, growing along the banks of the Nile River.

The production process of papyrus is a huge leap in the history of storage media evolution:

```
The art of making papyrus (3000 BC)
==================================

Material: Papyrus stems (Cyperus papyrus)
Tools: Knife, stone hammer, smooth stone or shell

Process steps:
1. Peeling → Peel off the outer skin of the papyrus stem to reveal the white pith
2. Cut → Cut the pith into thin slices about 30-40 cm long
3. Soak → Soak in water for several days to remove sugar and increase flexibility
4. Weaving → lay one layer of thin sheets lengthwise and another layer crosswise (similar to the cross-grain of today’s plywood)
5. Pressing → Beat repeatedly with a stone hammer to squeeze out the water and fuse the fibers
6. Drying → Drying in the sun, the natural gum in the plant fiber acts as an adhesive
7. Polishing → Using stones or shells to smooth the surface for writing
```

The finished product is an off-white sheet with a satin-smooth surface that can be rolled up, making it lightweight and easy to carry. The standard size of a piece of papyrus is about 40cm × 30cm, which is dozens of times larger than the capacity of a clay tablet.

This is not just a new medium. It is the first time that humans have created a storage medium, using chemical and mechanical means to process a plant into a brand new writing surface, instead of just "finding" a ready-made stone, a rope, or a piece of mud. From collection to manufacturing, this cognitive leap is no less than that from stone tools to bronze tools.

The advantages of Papyrus are revolutionary:
- Lightweight: What can be recorded on a roll of papyrus would require dozens of clay tablets
- Scrollable: A scroll can hold dozens of pages of content, making it extremely convenient to carry and store.
- High capacity density: For the same weight of media, the information density of papyrus is hundreds of times that of clay tablets

But papyrus also has its own "original sin."

---

## The Fragility of Papyrus

Papyrus is afraid of two things: humidity and insect infestation.

In the dry Egyptian desert, papyrus can be preserved for thousands of years, and we can still see ancient Egyptian papyrus rolls from four thousand years ago today. But once removed from a dry climate, papyrus will mold, rot, and decompose in a humid environment in just a few months. The hundreds of thousands of papyrus volumes in the Library of Alexandria were eventually destroyed by fire and time, and nothing remained.

Another problem is structural brittleness. Papyrus cannot be folded, only rolled. Once the scroll breaks, the entire document falls apart. In contrast, if a clay tablet is dropped to the ground, it will break into a few pieces at most, and you can still put it back together, because the indentation of cuneiform writing is three-dimensional, and the fracture surface will not destroy the integrity of the symbol.

```
Storage media durability comparison

Clay Tablets ─────→ ════════════════════════════════ Thousands of years (the fire strengthened it)
Papyrus ──→ ═══════════════════ Thousands of years (dry environment) / few months (moist)
Parchment ──→ ═══════════════════════════ One thousand to two thousand years
Paper ────→ ════════════════ for hundreds of years (acid-free paper can last for thousands of years)
Flash ───→ ════ ten years (data retention at room temperature)
DRAM ───→ blink of an eye (millisecond refresh interval)
```

Papyrus exchanged a fragile body for a leap in information density. This is a pattern that has persisted throughout the history of storage: **The birth of each new medium renegotiates the balance between durability and convenience.**

But you may notice a detail: Although papyrus is lighter than clay tablets, its manufacturing material depends on a specific plant. Papyrus only grows in a few areas in the Nile River Basin and the eastern coast of the Mediterranean. This limited the spread of the papyrus. The Library of Alexandria could hold hundreds of thousands of volumes, but the Roman Empire had to import papyri from Egypt. When Egypt stopped exporting papyri, writing in Europe came to a standstill.

Literal "supply chain disruption" that happened three thousand years ago.

## The Independent Path of the East: Oracle Bones, Bronze, and Bamboo Slips

While the clay tablets and papyrus in the Mediterranean evolved independently, the Chinese civilization in the East embarked on a completely independent evolutionary path of storage media.

**Oracle Bones (Shang Dynasty, about 1600-1046 BC)**

The Shang Dynasty people did not use clay, they used tortoise shells and ox shoulder blades. The diviner drilled grooves into the oracle bones and burned them with red-hot copper rods. The oracle bones cracked due to heat, and the word "divination" is the pictogram of the cracks. The direction of the crack was interpreted as the god's answer, and the scribe then carved the inscription with a knife:

"Guisi divination, Zhen: Is it raining today?"

(Guisi day divination, ask: Will it rain today?) What is stored on the oracle bones is the earliest human "database query", that is, querying the gods about the future weather, the outcome of wars, and the quality of harvests. And these "query results" were carved on bones and tortoise shells, buried underground, waiting to be seen again in three thousand years.

Oracle is the earliest mature Chinese character system we can see today. Of the roughly 4,500 characters, about 2,000 can already be deciphered. They are the origins of Chinese characters, which remain the most widely used writing system in the world.

But tortoise shells and animal bones are not good storage media. They are small in area, irregular in shape, and difficult to arrange flatly. The essential limitation of Oracle storage is that it is a **religious tool** rather than an **information management tool**. Its purpose was to record the results of an act of divination rather than to preserve and disseminate knowledge.

**Bronze Inscription (Western Zhou Dynasty, ca. 1046-771 BC)**

After the Yin and Shang Dynasties, the Zhou people moved Chinese characters to bronze vessels.

The Mao Gong Tripod is the longest existing bronze inscribed vessel. A 497-character inscription is cast on the inner wall, recording the appointment of King Xuan of Zhou to his minister Mao Gong. The casting process is extremely complicated: first, inscribe the reverse characters on the clay mold, turn it into a mold, and then pour liquid copper into it. After cooling, the clay mold is broken, and the inscription is permanently embedded in the bronze.

The durability of the bronze inscription is nearly infinite. As long as it is not melted and recast, bronze can be stored in the soil for thousands of years without losing its writing. But the cost is extremely high. Casting a bronze requires mining, smelting, mold making, and heating, each of which is a national-level project. Bronze is not a "storage medium", it is a "monument".

**Bamboo Slips and Wooden Slips (Warring States to Han Dynasty, approximately 475 BC - 200 AD)**

Finally, Eastern writers found a practical and cheap material, bamboo.

Bamboo can be found everywhere. It is split and cut into flat strips, then roasted over a fire to remove the moisture (this step is called "finalization", which is where the "film and television finalization" we talk about today comes from), and then written with a brush dipped in ink. The bamboo slips are strung together with leather ropes or hemp ropes and rolled into a tube shape. This is the original meaning of the word "book": the pictogram of bamboo slips strung together.

```
"Storage Parameters" of Bamboo Slips

Simple size: about 1cm wide × 23cm long (Han Simplified Chinese standard)
Simple capacity: about 20-40 words (depending on font size)
Capacity of one volume: dozens of slips strung together, hundreds to thousands of words
Weight density: A "Historical Records" (520,000 words) requires about 13,000 bamboo slips and weighs about 50 kilograms
Modification after writing: Scrape off the ink with a scraper ("delete" operation), or discard the sketch directly ("replace" operation)
```

It is said that Qin Shihuang had to review 120 kilograms of bamboo slips every day. In the Qin Dynasty, one kilogram weighed about 250 grams, which is about 30 kilograms, which is equivalent to more than 200,000 words. Dongfang Shuo wrote a letter to Emperor Wu of the Han Dynasty. The memorial used three thousand bamboo slips and required two guards to carry it into the palace.

This makes us deeply aware of how scarce "convenience" is in the evolution of storage media. To record a message on bamboo slips, you need to chop bamboo, break bamboo pieces, scrape the green bark, bake it, punch holes, weave rope, and then write it word by word. After the entire process, it may take several days to write a thousand-word document.

And its only natural advantage is: the materials are almost free, and they are everywhere in the mountains and plains of the south.

## Silk Book: Database on Silk

At the same time as the bamboo slips, a luxury storage medium was also in use, and that was silk.

The silk books unearthed from the Mawangdui Han Tomb allow us to see the "high-end storage" of the Han Dynasty people. On the light yellow silk, "Laozi", "Book of Changes" and "Warring States Policy" are written in ink, with exquisite fonts and neat layout, some with vermilion columns.

The advantages of silk books are overwhelming:
- Large area: A complete piece of silk can be used to write thousands of words without the need for splicing.
- Lightweight: The weight of silk and silk with the same content is less than one percent of that of bamboo slips
- Flat: Unlike bamboo slips, which are limited by the strip area, charts and maps can be drawn
- Foldable: only palm-sized after folding, extremely convenient to carry

But the price is also obvious: silk is too expensive. In the Han Dynasty, a piece of silk could be exchanged for several stones of grain. Ordinary scholars can’t afford it. There are only two target users of silk books: nobles and high-ranking officials.

The juxtaposition of bamboo slips and silk books constitutes the first "layered storage architecture" in the history of storage media. Bamboo slips are cheap mass storage (similar to today's HDD), and silk books are high-speed precious storage (similar to today's SSD). Bamboo slips are used for daily records and general documents, and silk books are used for important documents and ancient classics.

This is actually what we do in embedded systems today, putting frequently changing data in the cache and writing large blocks of file data to ordinary disks. **The idea of ​​hierarchical storage was already practiced by the Chinese two thousand years ago.**

---### Cai Lun’s papermaking: the first democratization of storage

In 105 AD, Cai Lun, the Shangfang Order of the Eastern Han Dynasty, presented an invention to Emperor He of the Han Dynasty that rewrote human history.

Textbooks have talked too much about papermaking, but they have missed a key point: **Cai Lun was not the first person to make paper**. Paper already existed in the Western Han Dynasty. Archaeologists discovered Western Han Dynasty paper in Fangmatan, Gansu Province, which was nearly two hundred years earlier than Cai Lun.

So what's so revolutionary about Cai Lun?

The reason is that he solved the problem of raw material supply for papermaking.

Paper in the Western Han Dynasty had a single raw material, mainly hemp, with extremely low output and high cost. It is essentially just a low-end version of silk. Cai Lun’s formula is completely different:

## Cai Lun's Papermaking: The First Democratization of Storage

```
The raw material revolution of Cai Lun’s papermaking

Bark + hemp + rag + fishing net
 ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑
After felling, after making rope, after wearing out, after fishing
scraps, crumbs, tatters, waste
```

None of the four raw materials require specialized production. Bark is a waste from wood processing, hemp is a scrap from rope making, rags are old clothes, and fishing nets are used garbage. They are themselves by-products of other production activities.

The reason why this formula can change the world is that its raw material cost is close to zero, and the process of soaking, pounding, beating, papermaking, and drying is not essentially different from papyrus. From then on, paper was no longer a luxury, and any village could make paper from rags.

The chain reaction brought about by this is unprecedented in scale.

## Typography: Storage from "Save" to "Copy"

Paper has solved the problem of "where to write", but there is still a huge bottleneck that has not been broken through: **how to write**?

In the medieval codex era, every book was a unique copy. The monks were in the scriptorium of the monastery, dipping their quills in ink and copying the parchment one stroke at a time. Copying a book can take months or even a year. A Bible is worth as much as a vineyard.

The efficiency of information dissemination is limited to an extremely low level. The inheritance of culture is essentially a one-way transmission from one person to another. Each generation needs to re-copy the classics of the previous generation, and errors, omissions and tampering are bound to occur in this process.

Block printing changed this pattern: a page of text was engraved on a wooden board, brushed with ink, spread with paper, pressed, and a hundred pages were printed, or a thousand pages were printed. The content is exactly the same.

```
Three major leaps in storage evolution

1. From dictation to writing → information exists independently without the person
   Oral tradition → clay tablets/papyrus/bamboo slips
   "Information no longer relies on the narrator's memory"

2. From writing to printing → information can be copied and spread independently
   Manuscript → Block printing → Movable type printing
   "One book can become a hundred thousand books"

3. From printing to digital → Information is separated from the physical matrix
   Paper → Disk/Flash/Cloud
   "Information is no longer attached to any specific medium"
```

During the Qingli period of the Northern Song Dynasty (about 1040 AD), Bi Sheng invented movable type printing. Use clay to burn individual character molds, put them together during typesetting, and dismantle them after printing. They can be reused next time. This essentially decouples "information" from the "physical arrangement of the medium." The content of a book is no longer on a fixed wooden board, but on countless fonts that can be flexibly combined. This is the first time in history that humans have achieved random access and flexible reorganization of stored content.

However, Bi Sheng's movable type was not popular in the East because there are tens of thousands of Chinese characters and the workload of typesetting was huge. What really changed the history of movable type printing was in the West: the movable lead printing press invented by Gutenberg in the 15th century only needed to arrange a dozen letters of Latin, which was extremely efficient. Gutenberg's Bible was printed in 180 copies, each with 1,282 pages, and was completed in about two years. This was an amount of work that a hundred monks could not complete in a hundred years by hand.

**When printing moved information from "preservation" to "reproduction," the target architecture of storage completely changed.** From now on, storage no longer only pursues "durability", because you have a copy, even if the original is burned, it doesn't matter. Storage began to pursue "diffusion": making copies faster and cheaper, and distributing them further to more people.

Copying, this action itself is a double-edged sword. On the one hand, copying means you can back up data and distribute knowledge at will. On the other hand, copying also means confusion, tampering, and forgery. **When you have a hundred copies, how do you know which one is "real"?** This leads to a new dimension of storage issues,**data integrity**, and this issue extends all the way to today's file system field, extending to the CRC32 check value you calculate in Flash.

## Libraries: From Clay Tablet Bookshelves to Digital Catalogs

Now, let us use the metaphor of “library” to review this ten-thousand-year evolution path. **Clay tablets are the cornerstone of the collection.** The most primitive, the bulkiest, but the most durable. A Sumerian library, its "bookshelf" is just a piece of wet mud slabs slapped on the mud floor. No tools or skills are needed, it is all done by hands. But this library cannot be moved, nor can the catalog be changed. Cuneiform writing is finalized as soon as it is engraved on it. You cannot "modify" the record on a clay tablet. It is a write-once, permanently solidified storage. We call it WORM (Write Once Read Many) today. Its durability is simply anti-human: you burn down the building, kill people, and destroy civilization, and the clay tablets from four thousand years ago are dug out of the sand, but the writing remains the same. This is a "lifespan" that no modern storage medium can match. But if your library needs to be relocated, sorry, Clay Tablet can't help you.

**Pyrus is a lightweight loan card.** Lightweight and can be placed where needed. A roll of paper is a mobile borrowing record that you can take with you. But its durability depends on the tolerance of the environment. In dry Egypt, it outlasted the clay tablets; in humid Rome, it expired in a few weeks. When you operate a library, you must take into account the local climate: this is exactly the same as Flash's "data retention depends on temperature". There is always an irremovable coupling between the physical characteristics of storage media and the environment.

**Bamboo slips are standardized bookshelf grids.** Standardization is the greatest contribution of bamboo slips. A bamboo slip is a bookshelf grid with a fixed length, fixed width, and fixed capacity. Multiple bamboo slips are assembled into one volume. You have a collection project, and you can accurately estimate how much bamboo and how much leather rope you will need. This idea of ​​"fixed units" directly influenced the later sector division of disks and the block management of Flash: dividing the storage space into equal-sized units is a basic means of managing complexity. Just replace one when it wears out, there is no need to tear down the entire bookshelf and start over.

**Paper is for printed catalog cards.** Cheap, mass-produced, standardized. Cai Lun's formula makes it almost free to run library materials, which means you can manage many collections and manage a large library. The birth of printing meant that you could not only hand-copy each book one by one, but you could rely on engraving to instantly copy hundreds or thousands of identical catalog cards. After this round of evolution, libraries are no longer a luxury for a few people, but have become an infrastructure that ordinary people can have. The spread of knowledge and information finally broke down class barriers.

**Modern Flash is a high-density digital archive.** Its physical characteristics are finely regulated by engineers (the manufacturing process determines the accuracy of the threshold voltage), its life model is accurately described by mathematical formulas (the relationship between erasure wear and the number of writes), and its usage management is scheduled by complex file system algorithms (wear leveling, garbage collection, bad block management). What readers see is just a "file", but behind this file is a complete set of project management for the entire library, from building bookshelves to cataloging to power outage protection.

From clay tablets to Flash, the five thousand years of human storage history are nothing but repeated answers to these three questions:
1. How to get information out of your mind? (encoding)
2. How long can it be kept after moving out? (durable)
3. How to find it when you need it? (search)

And every time a new medium is used to answer these three questions, it also brings new questions. Clay tablets are durable but heavy, papyrus is light but fragile, paper is cheap but flammable, and flash can be rewritten but has limited wear. **This exchange game never ends.**
